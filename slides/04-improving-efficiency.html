The equality concerns expectations, not equality of individual outputs or output distributions. An alternative contribution may include importance weights, controls, or a conditional expectation. The self-normalized IS subsection later introduces a ratio estimator as a separate extension, not a fixed equal-mean contribution average. Its sampling law belongs with g. Other equal-mean contributions may change the distribution and variance. The function h includes that contribution and all transforms from the uniform input. If the input is already uniform, S is the identity and h=g. Different constructions may require different cube dimensions. Self-normalized importance sampling

Importance
Sampling

Suppose the target normalizer is unknown

\[\begin{align*} C_{\mathrm{tar}}&=\int\widetilde\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(\vx)\,\dif\vx,& \varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(\vx)&=\frac{\widetilde\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(\vx)}{C_{\mathrm{tar}}} \end{align*}\]

For \(\vZ_0,\ldots,\vZ_{n-1}\ \IIDsim\ \varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}\), use

\[ \widetilde w(\vz)=\frac{\widetilde\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(\vz)} {\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}(\vz)}=C_{\mathrm{tar}}w(\vz) \]

\[ \hmu_{\mathrm{SN},n} =\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(\vZ_i)\widetilde w(\vZ_i)} {\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\widetilde w(\vZ_i)} \]

  • Any common positive weight multiplier cancels
  • Require proposal coverage of the target support and a positive denominator

A ratio of sample averages handles the unknown normalization

Owen, Section 9.2, calls this self-normalized importance sampling; the same ratio appears in equation (11.27). Assume 0<C_tar<infinity, IID proposal draws, full target-support coverage up to null sets, and integrability of f under the target. Unlike ordinary IS for a single integrand, the denominator requires coverage even where f is zero but the target density is positive. The denominator can be zero at finite n when every proposed weight is zero; the estimator is defined only when it is positive.

The proposal is normalized in the visible formula. If only an unnormalized proposal is evaluable but sampling from its normalized law is available, use tilde_varrho_tar/tilde_varrho_prop instead. The resulting weights differ by a common positive constant, which also cancels. Self-normalization estimates a normalized expectation, not the unnormalized integral of f times the target.

When the normalized density ratio is computable, ordinary IS remains available and is unbiased for integrable f. Both individual normalizers need not be known: a known ratio suffices. Self-normalization is still an option, with different finite-sample properties; neither estimator universally dominates. This ratio method belongs to IS, independently of MCMC, and does not turn proposal draws into target draws.

Posterior means by likelihood weighting

Recall Bayes’ theorem with likelihood \(L(\theta;y_{\mathrm{obs}})\) and prior density \(\varrho_{\mathrm{prior}}\)

\[ \mu_{\mathrm{post}}=\Ex(\Theta\mid y_{\mathrm{obs}}) =\frac{\int\theta L(\theta;y_{\mathrm{obs}})\varrho_{\mathrm{prior}}(\theta)\,\dif\theta} {\int L(\theta;y_{\mathrm{obs}})\varrho_{\mathrm{prior}}(\theta)\,\dif\theta} \]

Draw \(Z_i\ \IIDsim\ \varrho_{\mathrm{prior}}\) and use likelihood weights

\[ \hmu_{\mathrm{post},n} =\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}Z_iL(Z_i;y_{\mathrm{obs}})} {\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}L(Z_i;y_{\mathrm{obs}})} \]

  • The unknown evidence \(\varrho_D(y_{\mathrm{obs}})\) cancels
  • A posterior concentrated in a small part of the prior can make weights very uneven

Prior proposals with likelihood weighting give self-normalized IS

This uses the same likelihood, prior, posterior, and evidence notation as the Bayesian-inference slide in Markov Chain Monte Carlo. Here the proposal is the prior, so the unnormalized target is tilde_varrho_tar(theta)=L(theta;y_obs)varrho_prior(theta), the target normalizer C_tar is the evidence varrho_D(y_obs), and the unnormalized importance weight is just L(theta;y_obs). The proposal draws are named Z_i to preserve their role; their distribution is the prior, not the posterior. Require finite positive evidence, an integrable posterior first moment, and a positive sample denominator. The same construction estimates any integrable posterior expectation by replacing Z_i in the numerator by f(Z_i).

The exact posterior mean is a ratio of integrals. The displayed numerical estimator is self-normalized IS because it estimates those integrals using prior proposals. It is generally biased at finite n and consistent under the preceding assumptions. If IID posterior draws are available instead, their ordinary sample mean is unbiased for the posterior mean. An ordinary average of MCMC posterior states is a different estimator too; it does not become self-normalized IS merely because its target is a posterior.

For a two-point illustration, take prior probabilities 1/2,1/2 on theta=0,1 and likelihood values 1,3. The posterior probabilities are 1/4,3/4, giving posterior mean 3/4. With one prior proposal the likelihood cancels, so the displayed estimate has expectation 1/2. This makes the finite-sample bias concrete without requiring any MCMC construction. A better proposal can help when prior proposals rarely reach the concentrated posterior.

Bias and consistency

Two sample averages estimate two integrals

\[\begin{align*} \frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(\vZ_i)\widetilde w(\vZ_i) &\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{a.s.}}C_{\mathrm{tar}}\mu\\ \frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\widetilde w(\vZ_i) &\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{a.s.}}C_{\mathrm{tar}}>0 \end{align*}\]

Hence \(\hmu_{\mathrm{SN},n}\to\mu\) almost surely under the stated conditions

  • Generally biased at finite \(n\): unbiased components do not imply an unbiased ratio
  • Exact for constants: \(f\equiv c\) gives \(\hmu_{\mathrm{SN},n}=c\)
  • A ratio estimator needs its own bias and variance analysis

\(\exstar\) Target density \(2x\) on \((0,1)\), uniform proposal, and \(f(x)=x\) For \(n=1\), simplify \(\hmu_{\mathrm{SN},1}\) and compare its expectation with \(\mu\)

The strong law applies separately to the numerator and denominator sample averages; their limits are C_tar times mu and C_tar. The ratio then converges almost surely. Positive C_tar makes the sample denominator eventually positive almost surely, though an initial finite sample can have zero denominator.

For the exercise, tilde_varrho_tar(x)=x is enough, so tilde_w(z)=z. A uniform proposal lies in (0,1) almost surely and its weight is positive. With n=1, the weight cancels and the estimate is Z_0. Its expectation is 1/2, whereas the target mean is 2/3; the bias is -1/6. More observations give consistency, not a claim of exact finite-sample unbiasedness or a universal variance gain.

The sample-normalized weights are nonnegative and sum to one, so the estimate is a weighted average of the observed f values and integrates constants exactly. Statements about finite-sample bias assume the estimator’s expectation exists. When the relevant moments exist, MSE = variance + bias squared. The earlier equal-mean IID contribution formulas and their variance-only cost comparison do not apply automatically to this ratio estimator. No confidence interval or stopping rule for the ratio is asserted here.

Acceptance–rejection with a fixed proposal budget

Use independent proposals and a fixed valid envelope
Let \(I_i\) indicate acceptance and \(K_N=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}I_i\)

\[ \hmu_{\mathrm{AR},N} =\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}I_i f(\vZ_i)}{K_N},\qquad K_N>0 \]

This is normalization by a sample total, with zero–one weights

Stopping rule Accepted-sample average
Fix \(n\) acceptances Unbiased average of \(n\) IID target draws
Fix \(N\) proposals Unbiased conditional on \(K_N>0\)

If \(K_N=0\), the ratio is undefined

A random denominator does not by itself imply bias

Let tilde_w=tilde_varrho_tar/varrho_prop, and use a known fixed envelope M >= sup tilde_w with independent uniform decisions. Then Prob(I_i=1 | Z_i)=tilde_w(Z_i)/M and the unconditional acceptance probability is a=C_tar/M. Assume 0<a<=1 and integrable f under the target. Conditional on any specified acceptance pattern, the accepted proposals are independent and each has the normalized target law. Therefore, for every k>0, E(hatmu_AR,N | K_N=k)=mu, and E(hatmu_AR,N | K_N>0)=mu. This conditional unbiasedness is a special property of rejection sampling, so random sample count alone does not establish ratio-estimator bias.

Here K_N is binomial(N,a), so Prob(K_N=0)=(1-a)^N. The ratio itself is undefined on that event. If an implementation returns a fixed value b when no proposal is accepted, its unconditional bias is (b-mu)(1-a)^N. For zero fill this is -mu(1-a)^N. Discarding an empty batch and trying again gives the conditional unbiased average, but then the overall proposal count is random. Fixing the number n of accepted draws also gives an unbiased target average, with a random proposal count.

The connection to ordinary self-normalized IS is algebraic: replacing each acceptance indicator I_i by its conditional mean tilde_w(Z_i)/M in both sums gives the self-normalized importance ratio. The ratio of conditional expectations is not generally the conditional expectation of the ratio. Do not infer a Rao–Blackwell variance inequality or exact unbiasedness for SNIS from that replacement. In particular, the one-proposal bias example on the preceding slides remains valid.

Owen, Section 9.5, writes the fixed-proposal rejection-sampling average as a ratio with a random number of acceptances. Keep the zero-acceptance event explicit when comparing it with self-normalized IS. The fixed-envelope and independence assumptions matter; no adaptive rejection scheme is claimed here.

\[\begin{align*} \hmu_{\mathrm{SN},T,n} &=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f\bigl(\vT(\vZ_i)\bigr)\widetilde w_T(\vZ_i)} {\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\widetilde w_T(\vZ_i)}\\ &=\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f\bigl(\vT(\vZ_i)\bigr) \end{align*}\]

An exact map generates target samples without separately knowing \(C_{\mathrm{tar}}\)

The source/proposal density is normalized. Only the target normalizing constant is unknown. Throughout the visible slide, T is an exact transport from the source to the normalized target, not an approximate fitted map. The exact map encodes normalization; constructing it can be difficult.

This follows directly from the earlier change-of-variables formula: the normalized transport correction is w_T=1, so the unnormalized correction is C_tar. Both sums contain the same factor C_tar. Integrable f and IID source draws therefore give an ordinary unbiased target average in this constant-weight case. General nonconstant-weight self-normalization is generally biased at finite n.

The existing triangular example works without supplying the target normalizer as a separate input. With tilde_varrho_tar(x)=x on (0,1), a uniform source, and T(z)=sqrt(z), the unnormalized correction is sqrt(z)/(2 sqrt(z))=1/2, which equals C_tar. In contrast, the identity map has tilde_w(z)=z and gives the nonconstant-weight ratio from the preceding exercise. An arbitrary positive multiplier on the target shape changes only the common correction constant, not the normalized distribution or exact map.

For a general one-dimensional target, the quantile map uses the ratio of the cumulative integral of tilde_varrho to its total integral. That ratio is insensitive to an arbitrary density scale, but computing and inverting it can still require difficult global information. In higher dimensions, maps can be fitted against an unnormalized target without separately computing its normalizer. An approximate map need not produce exact target draws. A fitted flow generally approximates the target; exactness must not be inferred merely from calling the map a transport or normalizing flow. Importance weighting or Metropolis correction can address the mismatch, with the appropriate finite-sample qualifications.

Supporting reference: Marzouk, Moselhy, Parno, and Spantini, Sections 3.1–3.3. The existence and the practical construction of an exact transport are different questions. Same IID proposal points, different masses: approximate the target by reweighting

Weight the empirical distribution or weight the function: the same estimate

Motivation: an ordinary empirical distribution of the IID proposals approximates the proposal law, whereas we want target expectations and probabilities. Importance weighting changes the probability mass assigned to each observed point to account for the target/proposal density ratio. Normalizing those masses gives a discrete probability distribution that can be reused for different functions without changing the sampled points. For f equal to an indicator of a set A, the displayed integral estimates the target probability of A; other choices estimate target means or moments. This is not a construction specifically for repeated data: distinct points can receive unequal masses. For weights 1, 2, 7 the masses are 0.1, 0.2, 0.7. At finite n, the weighted empirical distribution generally differs from the target distribution.

Recall Markov Chain Monte Carlo: empirical distributions. Use the same \(\widehat F_n\) and point-mass \(\delta_{\vx_i}\) notation as the empirical-distribution development in Markov Chain Monte Carlo, with the subscript \(w\) recording the weights. The points still have the proposal law; weighting changes their empirical masses, not their locations or sampling law. The estimator converges almost surely to mu under the support and integrability assumptions stated on the self-normalization slides. The weights need only be proportional to the normalized density ratio. The denominator can be zero at finite \(n\) if every sampled weight is zero.

The displayed equality is exact for each sample with positive total weight. The function g_n depends on the whole sample through its denominator; it is not the fixed ordinary-IS contribution g=f w. Both Fhat_n and Fhat_n,w on this slide are probability distributions. Without self-normalization, ordinary IS assigns masses w(Z_i)/n, whose total generally differs from one. Ordinary importance sampling is unbiased for integrable \(f\); self-normalization produces a probability measure but generally loses finite-sample unbiasedness. The weighted-distribution view also connects integration error to discrepancy: the error is the integral of f against Fhat_n,w minus F_tar. Under the appropriate RKHS assumptions, its absolute value is bounded by the RKHS norm of f times the kernel discrepancy between these two distributions. This bound uses fixed f; applying a bound to the sample-dependent g_n requires care and is not a fixed-integrand argument. Full support coverage is needed to approximate the whole target distribution, which is stronger than covering only the support of \(f\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}\) for one particular integral. A weighted empirical approximation to the target also does not imply that the scalar importance contribution \(f(\vZ)w(\vZ)\) has the same distribution as \(f(\vX)\).

This is a deterministic inequality for every realized sample with positive total weight. Assume a measurable positive definite kernel with finite diagonal at every sample point and bounded integration against the normalized target on H_K. Optimal coefficients from covariances

\(Y=f(\vX)\), \(\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{\eta}(\vX)\), \(\Ex(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\vzero\)

Define the control covariance matrix and response–control covariances:

\[\begin{align*} \mSigma_\eta&=\cov(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\eta}),& (\mSigma_\eta)_{jk}&=\cov(\eta_j,\eta_k),\\ \boldsymbol{\kappa}&=\cov(\boldsymbol{\eta},Y),& \kappa_j&=\cov\{\eta_j(\vX),f(\vX)\} \end{align*}\]

\[\begin{align*} \var(Y_{\mathrm{CV}}) &=\Ex\bigl[((Y-\mu)-\vbeta^\mathsf T\boldsymbol{\eta})^2\bigr]\\ &=\var(Y)-2\vbeta^\mathsf T\boldsymbol{\kappa} +\vbeta^\mathsf T\mSigma_\eta\vbeta \end{align*}\]

The variance is a quadratic function of the coefficients

Minimize the control-variate variance

For nonsingular \(\mSigma_\eta\), differentiate and set the gradient to zero:

\[\begin{align*} \nabla_{\vbeta}\var(Y_{\mathrm{CV}}) &=-2\boldsymbol{\kappa}+2\mSigma_\eta\vbeta=\vzero,\\ \mSigma_\eta\vbeta_*&=\boldsymbol{\kappa},\qquad \vbeta_*=\mSigma_\eta^{-1}\boldsymbol{\kappa} \end{align*}\]

Completing the square confirms the minimum:

\[\begin{align*} \var(Y_{\mathrm{CV}}) &=\var(Y)-\boldsymbol{\kappa}^\mathsf T\mSigma_\eta^{-1}\boldsymbol{\kappa}\\ &\quad +(\vbeta-\vbeta_*)^\mathsf T\mSigma_\eta(\vbeta-\vbeta_*) \end{align*}\]

  • Minimum variance: \(\var(Y)-\boldsymbol{\kappa}^\mathsf T\mSigma_\eta^{-1}\boldsymbol{\kappa}\)
  • One control: \(\beta_*=\cov\{f(\vX),\eta_1(\vX)\}/\var\{\eta_1(\vX)\}\)

Assume finite second moments. A nonsingular covariance matrix is positive definite, so the final quadratic term is nonnegative and the minimizer is unique. With redundant controls, the covariance matrix can be singular; remove redundant controls or solve the normal equations with a pseudoinverse. The cross-covariance vector is in the covariance matrix’s range, and the optimal residual is unchanged by coefficient directions in its null space. At the optimum, cov(eta, Y_CV)=kappa-Sigma_eta beta_*=0: the residual is uncorrelated with every control. In practice, solve the covariance system rather than explicitly forming the inverse.

There is no general rule that the separate reductions in variance add, or that their reduction factors multiply. Each method acts on the contribution remaining after the previous changes. For example, importance sampling alters the covariance with a control, and conditioning can remove variation that a control was already explaining. Refit controls for the resulting contribution; under importance sampling, weight the control consistently and retain its correct known mean. Conditional expectations must use the appropriate law. Antithetic maps must preserve the sampling law to which they are applied.

For finite-variance contributions, exact conditioning cannot increase IID variance relative to the contribution being conditioned. Optimally chosen population control coefficients cannot increase IID variance because zero coefficients remain available. These guarantees do not make the separate gains additive or multiplicative, and do not guarantee lower computational cost. Estimated coefficients, an unsuitable importance proposal, or a sampling design that interacts poorly with the new integrand may give less benefit or worse performance. Low discrepancy performance depends on the resulting cube integrand and cannot be inferred from IID variance alone. Assess the final estimator, include pilot and transformation work, and use independent replications rather than within-design IID error formulas for dependent inputs.

Recall \(h(\vu)=g\bigl(\vS(\vu)\bigr)\), the complete calculation from uniform inputs

Each \(\vU_i\sim\Unif[0,1]^d\) but the points within a run are dependent

\[\begin{align*} \hmu_n(h)&=\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}h(\vU_i) &\Ex_{\mathrm{rand}}\{\hmu_n(h)\}&=\mu\\ \operatorname{MSE}\{\hmu_n(h)\} &=\var_{\mathrm{rand}}\{\hmu_n(h)\}\\ &=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n-1} \cov_{\mathrm{rand}}\{h(\vU_i),h(\vU_j)\} \end{align*}\]

  • IID points: off-diagonal covariances vanish
  • Randomized LD: the whole design determines the estimator variance

For unbiased RQMC, RMSE is the SD of the whole estimate

Estimate that SD using independent runs under Stopping criteria

Randomness here is in the points, with h fixed. It differs from the Gaussian-process average-case interpretation in Markov Chain Monte Carlo, where the points are fixed and the integrand is random. Marginal uniformity and integrability give unbiasedness. Finite second moments justify the covariance expansion. The diagonal terms sum to var{h(U)}/n, but RQMC also has off-diagonal terms. Do not discard them or substitute within-run sample variance divided by n as an RQMC variance estimate. Independent shifts or scrambles provide independent complete-run estimates. Their across-run sample variance estimates the variance of one whole run; divide by the number of runs only when reporting the variance of their mean. IID recap

Let \(s_n\) be the sample SD of the \(n\) IID outputs \(h(\vU_i)\)

\[ \hmu_n\ \pm\ t_{1-\alpha/2,n-1}\,\frac{s_n}{\sqrt n} \]

  • Normal IID outputs: exact \((1-\alpha)\) coverage at fixed \(n\)
  • CLT approximation: \(n\) large enough for the sample mean to be nearly normal
    Also need \(s_n\) to approximate the true SD well
  • QMCPy: CubMCCLT uses a two-stage CLT procedure with an inflated pilot SD
  • Other IID stopping criteria exploit known bounds on \(h\), using concentration inequalities

Compare the interval radius with \(\varepsilon\); justify adaptive stopping separately

Known bounds a <= h(U) <= b permit distribution-free concentration bounds such as Hoeffding’s inequality. Valid sequential stopping still needs an appropriate construction, such as a confidence sequence or allocated failure probabilities across checks; a fixed-n concentration bound alone does not automatically justify repeated checks. This aside does not claim that the listed QMCPy classes implement these bounded-output alternatives. The normality assumption concerns the scalar outputs h(U_i), not the uniform inputs U_i. The Student-t confidence interval is exact for a fixed n with independent identically distributed normal outputs and positive variance. This is the interval obtained by inverting the two-sided t-test for the mean. For general IID outputs with finite positive variance, a studentized CLT justifies approximate coverage asymptotically. At a practical finite n, the mean must be sufficiently close to normal and the sample SD sufficiently representative of the true SD. A large n alone does not diagnose rare tails. The normal critical value z_(1-alpha/2) is the CLT choice; the t critical value is also asymptotically valid but is not an exact finite-sample correction for nonnormal data. Neither fixed-n result automatically justifies repeatedly checking and stopping when the displayed radius first falls below epsilon. For RQMC, within-design values are dependent; replace the IID observations by independent whole-design estimates. The later replication slides give the formula and qualifications. CubMCCLT uses a normal quantile and an inflated pilot SD, not the exact fixed-n normal-data Student-t procedure. For that fixed-n interval, use scipy.stats.t.ppf(1-alpha/2, n-1) and the ordinary sample SD (ddof=1); QMCPy has no separate IID Student-t stopping class in this pinned version. An RMSE target and confidence coverage are distinct error criteria. Budget exhausted does not mean tolerance met.

RQMC error assessment by independent replications

QMCPy: CubQMCRepStudentT

Independently randomize the whole \(n\)-point design \(R\ge2\) times

\[\begin{align*} \hmu_n^{(r)}&=\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}h(\vU_i^{(r)}) &&r=0,\ldots,R-1\\ \hmu_{R,n}&=\frac1R\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}\hmu_n^{(r)} & \hat\sigma^2_{R,n}&=\frac1{R-1}\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}(\hmu_n^{(r)}-\hmu_{R,n})^2 \end{align*}\]

Quantity Meaning
\(\hat\sigma^2_{R,n}\) Estimates \(\sigma_n^2=\var_{\mathrm{rand}}\{\hmu_n(h)\}\) of one \(n\)-point run
\(\hat\sigma^2_{R,n}/R\) Estimates the variance of the reported mean \(\hmu_{R,n}\)

Our \(R\) observations are independent sample averages, not individual function values

Fix h, n, the design, and its randomization law across replications. The \(\hmu_n^{(r)}\) are IID and unbiased, with variance \(\sigma_n^2\) when second moments exist. Here \(\hmu_{R,n}\) averages \(R\) whole-run estimates, each based on \(n\) points, so the total point count is \(Rn\). The observations used in the sample-variance calculation are these R sample averages, not the individual outputs h(U_i) within them. The latter are dependent within a run. Then \(\Ex[\hat\sigma^2_{R,n}]=\sigma_n^2\) and \(\var(\hmu_{R,n})=\sigma_n^2/R\) exactly at fixed \(n\) and \(R\). The sample variance of the n function values within one randomized design is not a substitute: it does not account for their covariances. Each run uses an independent shift or scramble. Any fitted control must be fixed in advance or have an appropriate independent-pilot analysis. Source: Owen (2026), Section 5.6.

A stopping criterion from replications

For a fixed \(n\) and \(R\), an approximate \((1-\alpha)\) confidence interval is

\[ \hmu_{R,n}\ \pm\ t_{1-\alpha/2,R-1}\,\frac{\hat\sigma_{R,n}}{\sqrt R} \]

\(t_{1-\alpha/2,R-1}\) is a Student-\(t\) quantile; \(\alpha\) is the nominal failure probability

A candidate rule: increase \(n\) and recompute until \(t_{1-\alpha/2,R-1}\hat\sigma_{R,n}/\sqrt R\le\varepsilon\)

  • Small \(R\) in CubQMCRepStudentT: assume the \(\hmu_n^{(r)}\) are nearly normal
    This assumption is not always known to hold
  • Adaptive stopping: repeated checks do not automatically preserve \(1-\alpha\) coverage

Even exact fixed-\(n\) intervals need a separate justification for data-dependent stopping

This Student-t confidence interval is the interval counterpart of the two-sided t-test for the mean of the independent replicate estimates. Keeping R small relies on those whole-run estimates being close to normal; this assumption may not be established for the chosen integrand and randomization. At fixed n and R, this Student-t interval is exact for normal replicate means; otherwise it is an approximation whose adequacy depends on their distribution and R. Unbiasedness and finite variance alone do not make a small-R t interval exact. Larger n does not universally make an individual RQMC replicate asymptotically normal. For nested designs, grow each run’s n through admissible sizes, retaining its randomization. Recompute the independent run means and their across-run variance at the new n. A nominal fixed-sample interval does not automatically retain its coverage at a data-dependent stopping time. This is a separate issue from the normal/CLT approximation: even exact coverage at each fixed n does not imply the same coverage at a data-dependent n. Merely changing n on a predetermined schedule is not the problem; choosing when to report an interval using the observed estimates or widths is. Count all Rn function evaluations and the error-assessment work, including any discarded pilot work. Increasing R until an interval is narrow enough also requires a stopping-time coverage argument. A justified sequential procedure can address this, for example by allocating failure probabilities across a predetermined sequence of checks whose sum is at most alpha, provided each check has the claimed valid marginal coverage. Allocating alpha does not repair an unjustified t approximation. Time-uniform intervals are another possibility under suitable assumptions; ordinary fixed-sample t intervals do not automatically have that property. Why a small pilot does not determine RQMC error

\[\begin{align*} \text{IID:}\quad\var\{\hmu_n(h)\}&=\frac{\sigma^2(h)}n &\sigma^2(h)&=\var\{h(\vU)\}\\ \text{RQMC:}\quad\sigma_n^2&=\var_{\mathrm{rand}}\{\hmu_n(h)\} \end{align*}\]

  • IID: a pilot estimates \(\sigma^2(h)\), which is independent of \(n\)
  • RQMC: \(\hat\sigma^2_{R,n_0}\) estimates the variance of an \(n_0\)-point run
  • Changing \(n\) changes the design and the covariances within each run

A pilot at \(n_0\) estimates \(\sigma_{n_0}\); a large-\(n\) interval needs \(\sigma_n\)

Extrapolation needs an additional, justified scaling model

For IID contributions, an independent pilot estimates the same one-observation variance used in the final sample-mean formula for any n. Its uncertainty still matters, as in the earlier IID stopping discussion. For RQMC, the across-run sample variance at n0 targets sigma_n0 squared, not sigma_n squared for a larger design. There is no automatic IID scaling sigma_n = sigma_n0 sqrt(n0/n): the off-diagonal covariances also change. If an appropriate rate model and its validity range are known, extrapolation may be possible, but a single small design does not establish that model. To assess the new n directly, extend each independently randomized design and recompute its mean before estimating the across-run variance.
