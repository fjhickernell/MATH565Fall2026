From Sampling to Efficient Estimation

Variance
Reduction

Recap: How we reached target samples \(\vX\) and \(Y = f(\vX)\)

\(\vU\sim\Unif([0,1]^d),\quad \vZ\sim\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}},\quad \vX\sim\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}\)

  • Generating Samples: transport: \(\vZ=\vS(\vU)\), then \(\vX=\vT(\vZ)=\vT\bigl(\vS(\vU)\bigr)\); if \(\vS\) is the identity, \(\vX=\vT(\vU)\), E.g., quantile, affine
  • Generating Samples: acceptance–rejection: retain selected proposals as \(\vX\)
  • Markov Chain Monte Carlo: propose \(\vZ_i\mid\vX_i\); accept or repeat \(\vX_i\); target law at stationarity

Our plan here

Keep \(\mu=\Ex_{\mathrm{tar}}[f(\vX)]\) fixed, using two complementary levers:

  • First, change the contribution: replace \(Y=f(\vX)\) by an equal-mean alternative, such as a transformed or weighted \(g(\vZ)\), a control variate, or a conditional expectation
  • Then, change how samples are chosen: arrange uniform inputs in antithetic pairs, strata, or low discrepancy patterns before mapping them to target-space samples

Variance and variation reduction

Variance
Reduction

Keep the target quantity, but change its computational representation:

\[\begin{gather*} \vX\sim\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}},\quad Y=f(\vX),\qquad \vZ\sim\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}},\quad \widetilde Y=g(\vZ) \\ \alerttext{And} \quad \mu=\Ex(Y) = \Ex(\widetilde Y) \end{gather*}\]

Two sample means estimate the same \(\mu\):

\[\begin{align*} \hmu_{f,n}&=\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(\vX_i),& \hmu_{g,n}&=\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}g(\vZ_i) \end{align*}\]

  • Change the function, the sampling distribution, or both
  • Seek lower error for comparable computational work
  • Changing only the function gives the special case \(g(\vX)\)

Variance reduction

For IID samples and finite second moments, both sample means are unbiased:

\[\begin{align*} \var(\hmu_{f,n})&=\frac{\var_{\mathrm{tar}}\{f(\vX)\}}n,& \var(\hmu_{g,n})&=\frac{\var_{\mathrm{prop}}\{g(\vZ)\}}n \end{align*}\]

Their MSEs equal their variances; IID RMSE is \(\Order(n^{-1/2})\)

Equal means allow different output distributions and different variances

For randomized low discrepancy inputs, dependence changes the variance formula

The two methods need not be independent of one another for these individual variance formulas. Each method needs independent replicates internally. Compare computational costs as well as the variance of one contribution. Introduction showed that accepting some bias can reduce MSE; here we compare unbiased estimators. Degenerate zero-variance cases need no asymptotic rate.

Variation reduction for low discrepancy sampling

To compare designs on the unit cube, make the uniform inputs explicit:

\[\begin{align*} \vX&=\vT(\vU),& h_f(\vu)&=f\bigl(\vT(\vu)\bigr),\\ \vZ&=\vS(\vU),& h_g(\vu)&=g\bigl(\vS(\vu)\bigr) \end{align*}\]

Both cube integrands have integral \(\mu\); keep the nodes \(\vu_0,\ldots,\vu_{n-1}\) fixed

For \(h_g\in\mathcal H_K\) and uniform distribution \(F_{\mathrm{unif}}\),

\[ \left|\mu-\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}h_g(\vu_i)\right| \le D\bigl(\{\vu_i\}_{i=0}^{n-1},F_{\mathrm{unif}};K\bigr)\norm{h_g}_{\mathcal H_K} \]

  • Nodes determine discrepancy; the composed cube integrand determines variation
  • Seek a smaller \(\norm{h_g}_{\mathcal H_K}\) for the same nodes and kernel

The notation \(\vT\) identifies a transport from uniform inputs to the target; \(\vS\) generates proposal draws. Neither map must be displayed when uniform inputs are incidental. This common-cube comparison assumes compatible input dimensions; conditioning can reduce dimension. The bound is the Markov Chain Monte Carlo worst-case integration-error bound. For sufficiently smooth composed cube integrands, nested uniform scrambling gives variance nearly \(\Order(n^{-3})\) and RMSE nearly \(\Order(n^{-3/2})\). This is not a guarantee for every randomization or integrand. Owen (1997) provides the variance result.

The Keister example

Write the original integral as a target expectation:

\[\begin{gather*} \mu=\int_{\reals^d}\cos(\norm{\vx})\me^{-\norm{\vx}^2}\,\dif\vx =\Ex_{\mathrm{tar}}[f(\vX)],\\ \vX\sim\Norm(\vzero,\mI_d/2),\qquad f(\vx)=\mpi^{d/2}\cos(\norm{\vx}) \end{gather*}\]

Choose \(\vZ_a\sim\Norm(\vzero,\mI_d/a^2)\) and apply a density correction:

\[ g_a(\vz)=\frac{(2\mpi)^{d/2}}{a^d} \cos(\norm{\vz})\exp\bigl[-(1-a^2/2)\norm{\vz}^2\bigr] \]

Every \(a>0\) gives \(\Ex[g_a(\vZ_a)]=\mu\); changing \(a\) changes the contribution

First identify a probability density in the original integral. Here \(\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(\vx)=\mpi^{-d/2}\me^{-\norm{\vx}^2}\) is the \(\Norm(\vzero,\mI_d/2)\) density, so \(\me^{-\norm{\vx}^2}=\mpi^{d/2}\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(\vx)\). The factor \(\mpi^{d/2}\) belongs in \(f\) when the integral is written as \(\Ex_{\mathrm{tar}}[f(\vX)]\).

Keister on the unit cube

Set \(\vS_a(\vu)=\bigl(\Phi^{-1}(u_j)/a\bigr)_{j=1}^d\) and \(h_a(\vu)=g_a\bigl(\vS_a(\vu)\bigr)\)

With \(\vq(\vu)=\bigl(\Phi^{-1}(u_j)\bigr)_{j=1}^d\),

\[ h_a(\vu)=\frac{(2\mpi)^{d/2}}{a^d} \cos\bigl(\norm{\vq(\vu)}/a\bigr) \exp\bigl[-(2-a^2)\norm{\vq(\vu)}^2/(2a^2)\bigr] \]

\(\exstar\) Verify \(g_a=f\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}/\varrho_{\mathrm{prop},a}\) and \(\int_{[0,1]^d}h_a(\vu)\,\dif\vu=\mu\)

  • \(0<a^2\le2\): \(h_a\) is bounded; \(a=\sqrt2\) gives \(g_a=f\)
  • \(2<a^2<4\): \(h_a\) is unbounded but has finite variance, including \(a=1.6\)
  • \(a^2\ge4\): infinite variance

Choose \(a\) by estimator variance or cube-integrand variation

Every positive \(a\) preserves the mean. The second moment is \[ \Ex[g_a(\vZ_a)^2]=\frac{(2\mpi)^{d/2}}{a^d} \int_{\reals^d}\cos^2(\norm{\vz}) \exp\bigl[-(2-a^2/2)\norm{\vz}^2\bigr]\,\dif\vz. \] It is finite precisely when \(a^2<4\). For \(2<a^2<4\), the cube-boundary singularity is mild enough for square integrability. At \(a^2=4\), Gaussian decay disappears and the squared cosine cannot provide cancellation; for larger \(a^2\), the envelope grows. The notebook uses \(a=1.6\) to explore an unbounded integrand that still has finite variance.

Transforming an integral

Analysis
Importance
Sampling

Start with \(\displaystyle \mu=\Ex_{\mathrm{tar}}[f(\vX)]=\int_{\mathcal X}f(\vx)\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(\vx)\,\dif\vx\)

Choose an invertible differentiable map \(\vT:\mathcal Z\to\mathcal X\) and \(\vZ\sim\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}\) covering the required support

\[\begin{align*} g(\vz)&=f\bigl(\vT(\vz)\bigr)w_T(\vz),\\ w_T(\vz)&= \frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}\bigl(\vT(\vz)\bigr) \left|\det\nabla\vT(\vz)\right|} {\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}(\vz)} \end{align*}\]

Then \(\mu=\Ex_{\mathrm{prop}}[g(\vZ)]\), where \(\nabla\vT(\vz)\) is the Jacobian matrix of \(\vT\) and \(\left|\det\nabla\vT(\vz)\right|\) is its volume-scaling factor

A general change of variables preserves the integral through a correction weight

Assume the map and inverse meet the change-of-variables theorem’s conditions, and the proposal is positive wherever the transformed integrand is nonzero. The target density is normalized; integrability is required for the expectation. The transformed proposal need not have the specified target density. For a Lebesgue integral \(\int r(\vx)\,\dif\vx\), the same mechanism gives \(g(\vz)=r(\vT(\vz))|\det\nabla\vT(\vz)|/ \varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}(\vz)\) without introducing a target density.

Transforming a random variable

An exact transport \(\vT:\mathcal Z\to\mathcal X\) satisfies

\[ \varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}\bigl(\vT(\vz)\bigr) \left|\det\nabla\vT(\vz)\right| =\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}(\vz) \]

Then \(w_T(\vz)=1\) wherever \(\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}(\vz)>0\), and so \(\vX=\vT(\vZ)\sim\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}\), and

\[ g(\vz)=f\bigl(\vT(\vz)\bigr),\qquad g(\vZ)\stackrel d=f(\vX) \]

Exact transport preserves the whole output distribution, including IID variance

  • Provides access to target samples; construction and evaluation costs can differ
  • Different transports can change low discrepancy performance through the cube integrand

Importance sampling

Importance
Sampling

Choose \(\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}>0\) wherever \(f\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}\ne0\)

\[\begin{align*} w(\vz)&=\frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(\vz)}{\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}(\vz)},& g(\vz)&=f(\vz)w(\vz),\\ \mu&=\Ex_{\mathrm{tar}}[f(\vX)] =\Ex_{\mathrm{prop}}[g(\vZ)] \end{align*}\]

For \(\vZ_0,\ldots,\vZ_{n-1}\ \IIDsim\ \varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}\),

\[ \hmu_{\mathrm{IS},n}=\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}g(\vZ_i) =\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(\vZ_i)w(\vZ_i) \]

\(w\) is the weight function; \(W=w(\vZ)\) is its random value

One proposal, two unbiased estimators

\[\begin{gather*} \varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}(z)=1,\qquad \varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x)=2x,\qquad 0<x,z<1,\\ Z_0,\ldots,Z_{n-1}\ \IIDsim\ \Unif(0,1),\qquad \mu=\Ex_{\mathrm{tar}}[f(X)] \end{gather*}\]

Brownian motion with drift

Let \(\vX\sim\Norm(\vzero,\mSigma)\) be the target Brownian path, with \(\Sigma_{jk}=\min(t_j,t_k)\) and discounted payoff \(Y=f(\vX)\)

Choose \(\vZ\sim\Norm(\va,\mSigma)\), where \(\va=\theta(t_1,\ldots,t_d)^\mathsf T\)

\[\begin{align*} w(\vz)&=\frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(\vz)}{\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}(\vz)} =\exp\!\left(-\theta z_d+\frac{\theta^2t_d}{2}\right),\\ g(\vZ)&=f(\vZ)w(\vZ),\qquad \Ex_{\mathrm{prop}}[g(\vZ)]=\mu \end{align*}\]

  • Drift makes positive payoffs more common
  • Weights preserve the target expectation; a good drift can reduce variance

Control variates

Variance
Reduction

Let \(\eta_1,\ldots,\eta_m\) have known zero means

For fixed coefficients \(\vbeta\), define \(\widetilde Y=g(\vX)\) with

\[ g(\vx)=f(\vx)-\sum_{j=1}^m\beta_j\eta_j(\vx) \]

Then \(\Ex[g(\vX)]=\Ex[f(\vX)]=\mu\), so

\[ \hmu_{\mathrm{CV},n} =\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}g(\vX_i) =\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\left[f(\vX_i)-\sum_{j=1}^m\beta_j\eta_j(\vX_i)\right] \]

is unbiased

Choose \(\vbeta\) to make \(\var[g(\vX)]\) as small as possible

Choosing coefficients by least squares

Variance
Reduction
Linear
Algebra

Center the response and control values:

\[\begin{align*} y_i&=f(\vx_i),& c_i&=y_i-\overline y,\\ H_{ij}&=\eta_j(\vx_i)-\overline{\eta}_j \end{align*}\]

Then

\[ \hvbeta=\argmin_{\vb}\norm{\vc-\mH\vb}^2 \]

When \(\mH^\mathsf T\mH\) is nonsingular,

\[ \hvbeta=(\mH^\mathsf T\mH)^{-1}\mH^\mathsf T\vc \]

The residual is orthogonal to the column space of \(\mH\), so least squares removes the component of \(f\) explained by the controls

Practical control-variate choices

Variance
Reduction
  • Good controls are inexpensive, have known means, and are strongly related to \(f\)
  • More controls can reduce the sample size needed for a target accuracy
  • Poor controls may increase computation without reducing variance
  • For IID sampling, least squares targets variance reduction
  • For low discrepancy sampling, choose coefficients to reduce variation, not sampling variance

A control variate turns known expectations into information about an unknown expectation

Conditional Monte Carlo

Variance
Reduction
Probability

Let \(V\) be a conditioning variable
Recall the laws of total expectation and variance

\[\begin{align*} \Ex(Y)&=\Ex_V\{\Ex(Y\mid V)\},\\ \var(Y)&=\Ex_V\{\var(Y\mid V)\}+\var_V\{\Ex(Y\mid V)\} \end{align*}\]

Therefore

\[ \var_V\{\Ex(Y\mid V)\}\le\var(Y) \]

If \(Y=f(X_1,\ldots,X_d)\) and

\[ \widetilde Y=g(\vX_{2:d})=\Ex(Y\mid \vX_{2:d}) \]

is analytic, estimate \(\mu\) by sampling \(g(\vX_{2:d})\) instead of \(Y\)

Analytically averaging over part of the randomness reduces variance

Conditional density estimation

Density
Estimation

Write the simulator on uniform inputs as \(Y=h(\vU)\) with \(\vU\sim\Unif[0,1]^d\)

Suppose \(h\) is increasing in \(u_1\) and

\[ y=h(u_1,\vu_{2:d})\iff u_1=g(y;\vu_{2:d}) \]

Extend the inverse by clipping to \([0,1]\) outside the conditional support
Then

\[\begin{align*} F_{Y\mid \vU_{2:d}}(y\mid \vu_{2:d}) &=\Prob\{U_1\le g(y;\vu_{2:d})\}\\ &=g(y;\vu_{2:d}),\\ \varrho_Y(y) &=\int_{[0,1]^{d-1}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(y;\vu_{2:d})\,\dif\vu_{2:d} \end{align*}\]

Thus a density value becomes an expectation of a conditional density

Density of a sum of uniforms

Density
Estimation

Let

\[ Y=\gamma_1U_1+\cdots+\gamma_dU_d, \qquad \vU\sim\Unif[0,1]^d, \qquad \gamma_j>0 \]

Solving for \(u_1\) gives

\[ g(y;\vu_{2:d})=\frac{y-\sum_{j=2}^d\gamma_ju_j}{\gamma_1} \]

when \(\sum_{j=2}^d\gamma_ju_j\le y\le\gamma_1+\sum_{j=2}^d\gamma_ju_j\)

Hence

\[ \varrho_Y(y)=\Ex\!\left[ \frac1{\gamma_1}\indic\!\left\{ \sum_{j=2}^d\gamma_jU_j\le y\le \gamma_1+\sum_{j=2}^d\gamma_jU_j \right\}\right] \]

Histograms, KDE, and conditional density

Density
Estimation

Output samples \(Y_i\), equal-width bins \(I_k\) of width \(b\), kernel \(\kappa\) with \(\int\kappa=1\)

\[\begin{align*} \widehat\varrho_{\mathrm{Hist}}(y) &=\frac1{nb}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\indic\{Y_i\in I_k\},\quad y\in I_k\\ \widehat\varrho_{\mathrm{KDE}}(y) &=\frac1{nb}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\kappa\!\left(\frac{y-Y_i}{b}\right)\\ \widehat\varrho_{\mathrm{CMC}}(y) &=\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\varrho_{Y\mid V}(y\mid V_i) \end{align*}\]

Antithetic sampling

Variance
Reduction
Probability

If \(\vA(\vX)\) has the same distribution as \(\vX\), pair \(f(\vX_i)\) with \(f\bigl(\vA(\vX_i)\bigr)\):

\[ \hmu_{\mathrm{Anti},n} =\frac1{2n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\left[f(\vX_i)+f\bigl(\vA(\vX_i)\bigr)\right] \]

Examples (uniform-input pairing uses the composed integrand \(h\)):

\[\begin{align*} \vA(\vU)&=\vone-\vU,& \vU&\sim\Unif[0,1]^d,\\ \vA(\vX)&=-\vX,& \vX&\sim\Norm(\vzero,\mSigma) \end{align*}\]

For IID starting samples, \(\vX_i\) and \(\vA(\vX_i)\) are dependent within pairs; pairs are independent across \(i\)

The variance is

\[ \var(\hmu_{\mathrm{Anti},n}) =\frac{\var\{f(\vX)\}}{2n} \left[1+\corr\{f(\vX),f(\vA(\vX))\}\right] \]

Antithetic sampling helps when the paired function values are negatively correlated

Beyond the IID variance formulas

  • Map, weight, and control calculations add work beyond evaluations of \(f\)
  • For deterministic or randomized low discrepancy points, error depends on integrand variation and point structure; the IID formulas do not apply

Exact transport preserves the distribution of \(f(\vX)\), so it has the same IID variance as direct target sampling, though it can be better than weighted importance sampling for a particular \(f\). This does not say that every change of variables preserves variance: the Keister example changes the unit-cube integrand, including its Jacobian. The formulas assume finite second moments and independent replicates or independent antithetic pairs.

Latin hypercube sampling in one dimension

Variance
Reduction

For the cube integrand \(h\), place one independent uniform point in each of \(n\) equal strata:

\[ U_i=\frac{i+V_i}{n}, \qquad V_i\ \IIDsim\ \Unif[0,1], \qquad i=0,\ldots,n-1 \]

Define \(\hmu_{\mathrm{LHS},n}=n^{-1}\sum_i h(U_i)\)
Then \(U_i\sim\Unif[i/n,(i+1)/n]\) and

\[\begin{align*} \Ex(\hmu_{\mathrm{LHS},n}) &=\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\Ex\{h(U_i)\}\\ &=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\int_{i/n}^{(i+1)/n}h(u)\,\dif u\\ &=\int_0^1h(u)\,\dif u \end{align*}\]

The \(U_i\) are independent but not identically distributed

One-dimensional LHS variance

Because the strata are independent,

\[ \var(\hmu_{\mathrm{LHS},n}) =\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\var\{h(U_i)\} \]

For differentiable \(h\), the mean value and Taylor theorems imply

\[\begin{align*} \var(\hmu_{\mathrm{LHS},n}) &\le\frac{\norm{h'}_\infty^2}{n^3},\\ \rmse(\hmu_{\mathrm{LHS},n}) &\le\frac{\norm{h'}_\infty}{n^{3/2}} \end{align*}\]

In one dimension, stratification can improve the IID \(n^{-1/2}\) RMSE rate to \(n^{-3/2}\) for smooth \(h\)

Latin hypercube sampling in \(d\) dimensions

Let \(\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_d\) be independent random permutations of \(0,\ldots,n-1\):

\[ U_{i\ell}=\frac{\pi_\ell(i)+V_{i\ell}}n, \qquad V_{i\ell}\ \IIDsim\ \Unif[0,1] \]

Each point is \(\vU_i=(U_{i1},\ldots,U_{id})\); each coordinate uses every stratum exactly once

Random permutations make every \(\vU_i\) marginally uniform; the points are dependent

For \(R\) independent Latin hypercubes,

\[\begin{align*} \hmu_{\mathrm{LHS},n}^{(r)} &=\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}h(\vU_i^{(r)}),\\ \hmu_{\mathrm{LHS},n,R} &=\frac1R\sum_{r=1}^R\hmu_{\mathrm{LHS},n}^{(r)},\\ \widehat{\var}(\hmu_{\mathrm{LHS},n,R}) &=\frac1{R(R-1)}\sum_{r=1}^R \left(\hmu_{\mathrm{LHS},n}^{(r)}-\hmu_{\mathrm{LHS},n,R}\right)^2 \end{align*}\]

Strengths and limitations of LHS

Strengths

  • No importance density, control variate, or conditional expectation required
  • Can converge faster than IID sampling
  • Guarantees one point in every one-dimensional stratum

Limitations

  • The sample size is fixed when the design is constructed
  • Accuracy assessment requires independent replications
  • Too many replications can erase the efficiency gain
  • The one-dimensional \(n^{-3/2}\) RMSE guarantee does not extend automatically to \(d>1\)

The van der Corput sequence

Low
Discrepancy

Write a nonnegative integer in base \(b\):

\[ i=(\cdots i_2i_1i_0)_b \]

Reverse the digits across the radix point:

\[ \phi_b(i)=(0.i_0i_1i_2\cdots)_b =\frac{i_0}b+\frac{i_1}{b^2}+\frac{i_2}{b^3}+\cdots \]

  • Usually \(b\) is prime; \(b=2\) is common
  • \(\{\phi_b(i)\}_{i=0}^{b^m-1}\) contains the same points as \(\{i/b^m\}_{i=0}^{b^m-1}\) in a different order
  • Earlier points keep their order as the sample grows

The van der Corput construction turns a fixed-size grid into an extensible sequence

First points in base two

For \(i=0,1,2,3\), reversing the binary digits gives

\[ \bigl(\phi_2(0),\phi_2(1),\phi_2(2),\phi_2(3)\bigr) =\bigl(0,\frac12,\frac14,\frac34\bigr) \]

The first four points form the grid \(\{0,1/4,1/2,3/4\}\) in a different order

\(\exstar\) Compute \(\phi_2(9)\), \(\phi_2(14)\), and \(\phi_2(19)\) by reversing their binary digits

The new values are \(1/8\) and \(5/8\), respectively: reverse the three binary digits in \(4=100_2\) and \(5=101_2\) across the radix point. For the larger indices, \(9=1001_2\), \(14=1110_2\), and \(19=10011_2\) give \(\phi_2(9)=9/16\), \(\phi_2(14)=7/16\), and \(\phi_2(19)=25/32\).

Randomized low discrepancy sampling

Low
Discrepancy

Randomization aims for

\[ \vU_{\mathrm{rand},i}\sim\Unif[0,1]^d \]

for every \(i\), while preserving low discrepancy jointly

  • Random shift: \(\vU_{\mathrm{rand},i}=\vu_i+\vDelta\bmod1\), especially for lattices and Kronecker sequences
  • Random digital shift: \(\vU_{\mathrm{rand},i}=\vu_i\oplus\vDelta\), especially for digital sequences
  • Linear matrix or nested uniform scrambling, especially for digital sequences

Independent randomizations support error assessment without reverting to IID point placement; recall the earlier variance and RMSE comparison

Each randomized point is marginally uniform, so its sample mean is unbiased. Randomization almost surely avoids exact cube-boundary points, making Gaussian quantile transforms finite. The opening variance slide states the nearly \(\Order(n^{-3/2})\) RMSE rate for sufficiently smooth integrands under nested scrambling.

The component-by-component strategy:

  1. Choose a one-dimensional generator that minimizes a discrepancy criterion over relevant sample sizes
  2. Freeze the first \(\ell\) components
  3. Search for component \(\ell+1\) that minimizes discrepancy
  4. Increase \(\ell\) and repeat until \(\ell=d\)

Two practical questions are

\[\begin{align*} \abs{\mu-\hmu_n}&\le\text{what?}\\ \abs{\mu-\hmu_n}&\le\varepsilon \quad\text{for what $n$?} \end{align*}\]

We want answers based only on the computed contributions \(Y_i\) or \(\widetilde Y_i\)

  • IID sampling: use a Central Limit Theorem interval when justified
  • Randomized low discrepancy sampling: use independent replications
  • Deterministic low discrepancy sampling: possible criteria include
    • decay of estimated Fourier coefficients
    • Bayesian credible intervals based on a kernel model

An efficient method needs both fast error decay and a reliable stopping rule

Approximate intervals and conditional bounds need their stated assumptions

The Keister reference is checked only after stopping. The IID interval uses a pilot-sized final run; the replication interval uses independent scrambles, not the within-scramble sample variance. Nominal fixed-sample interval coverage does not automatically carry over to adaptive stopping. Walsh-decay guarantees assume cone membership, which passing necessary-condition checks cannot prove. For the Asian option, distinguish simulation error from model and discretization error, and include the pilot in the computational budget.
