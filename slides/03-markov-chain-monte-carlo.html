From a global sampling construction to a target-preserving transition

Under suitable conditions, the distribution of \(X_i\) approaches the target as \(i\to\infty\)

The Markov property

Probability

Let \(\mathcal X\) be the state space for \(X_0,X_1,\ldots\)

The sequence is a Markov chain when, for every measurable \(A\subseteq\mathcal X\),

\[\begin{equation*} \Prob(X_{i+1}\in A\mid X_i=x_i,\ldots,X_0=x_0)\\ =\Prob(X_{i+1}\in A\mid X_i=x_i) \end{equation*}\]

Where the chain goes next depends only on where it is now, not on how it arrived

We are interested in the asymptotic behavior of \(X_i\)

  • In MCMC, construct the chain so that, under suitable conditions, the distribution of \(X_i\) approaches the target as \(i\to\infty\)

Examples of Markov chains

Probability

Assume \(Z_0,Z_1,\ldots\ \IIDsim\ \Norm(0,1)\)

Markov

\[\begin{align*} X_0&=0,& X_{i+1}&=X_i+Z_i\\ X_0&=0,& X_{i+1}&=i+Z_i \end{align*}\]

The second chain is not time homogeneous: its transition law changes with \(i\)

Not Markov as written

\[ X_0=X_1=0, \qquad X_{i+1}=X_{i-1}+Z_i \]

The pair \(Y_i=(X_{i-1},X_i)\) is Markov because its state retains the needed history

Only the first is a martingale: its expected next value, given the history, equals its current value

The Metropolis–Hastings algorithm

Markov Chain
Monte Carlo

Suppose

  • the target has known, possibly unnormalized density \(\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}\)
  • \(\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}(z\mid x)\) proposes a new state \(z\) from the current state \(x\)

Given \(X_0\), repeat for \(i=0,\ldots,n-1\):

  1. Generate \(Z\sim\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}(\cdot\mid X_i)\) and \(U\sim\Unif[0,1]\)

  2. Compute

    \[ \alpha(X_i,Z)=\min\!\left\{1, \frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(Z)\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}(X_i\mid Z)} {\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(X_i)\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}(Z\mid X_i)}\right\} =\min\!\left\{1, \frac{\text{joint density of first $Z$, then $X_i$}} {\text{joint density of first $X_i$, then $Z$}}\right\} \]

  3. Set \(X_{i+1}=Z\) if \(U\le\alpha(X_i,Z)\)
    Otherwise set \(X_{i+1}=X_i\)

Every iteration accepts either the proposal or the current state
Nothing is discarded

Why the acceptance ratio works

Markov Chain
Monte Carlo
Probability

The ratio compares the two directions of a proposed transition:

\[ \frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(Z)\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}(X_i\mid Z)} {\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(X_i)\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}(Z\mid X_i)} = \frac{\text{target weight at $Z$}\times\text{return proposal}} {\text{target weight at $X_i$}\times\text{forward proposal}} \]

  • Moves toward higher target density are favored
  • Asymmetric proposals are corrected by the proposal-density ratio
  • The normalizing constant in \(\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}\) cancels

The transition kernel satisfies detailed balance with the target distribution, making the target invariant under suitable conditions

The Metropolis algorithm

Markov Chain
Monte Carlo

If the proposal is symmetric,

\[ \varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}(z\mid x) =\varrho_{\mathrm{prop}}(x\mid z), \]

then the acceptance probability simplifies to

\[ \alpha(x,z)=\min\!\left\{1, \frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(z)}{\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x)}\right\} \]

For a random-walk proposal \(Z=X_i+\epsilon_i\) with symmetric \(\epsilon_i\):

  • accept every move to a state with at least as much target density
  • accept some downhill moves, which keeps the chain from becoming trapped immediately
  • repeat \(X_i\) when a proposal is rejected

Tuning and diagnostics

Markov Chain
Monte Carlo
Statistics

MCMC is attractive because it

  • samples from complicated, unnormalized target densities
  • is often straightforward to implement

The proposal and starting point require care:

  • proposals that are too small explore slowly and produce highly dependent samples
  • proposals that are too large have low acceptance rates and many repeats
  • multimodal targets may trap a single chain in one mode

Useful responses include pilot runs, multiple chains, and parallel tempering

MetropolisHastings companion notebook

Diagnose both exploration of the full state space and concentration in high-density regions

Parallel tempering: explore hot, estimate cold

Run replicas with inverse temperatures \(1=\beta_0>\cdots>\beta_{R-1}>0\)

\[\begin{align*} \varrho_{\beta_r}(x)&\propto\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x)^{\beta_r} \end{align*}\]

  • Cold (\(\beta_0=1\)): original target; hot (\(\beta_r<1\)): flatter density, easier valley crossing
  • Each sweep: local Metropolis updates, then proposed state swaps between neighboring temperatures
  • Accept a swap \(x_r\leftrightarrow x_s\) with probability

\[\begin{align*} \alpha_{\mathrm{swap}} &=\min\left\{1, \left[\frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x_s)}{\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x_r)}\right]^{\beta_r-\beta_s}\right\} \end{align*}\]

  • Accepted swaps bring hot-chain discoveries into the cold slot
  • Use cold-slot samples only for the original target; inspect mode visits and swap rates

Temperature is \(T_r=1/\beta_r\). Raising the whole density to a power below one reduces peak-to-valley density ratios. Each tempered density must have a finite normalizing integral; this holds for the notebook’s Gaussian mixture.

The augmented target is the product of the \(R\) tempered densities. A symmetric proposal to exchange two states has MH ratio \[\begin{align*} \frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x_s)^{\beta_r} \varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x_r)^{\beta_s}} {\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x_r)^{\beta_r} \varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x_s)^{\beta_s}} &=\left[\frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x_s)} {\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x_r)}\right]^{\beta_r-\beta_s}. \end{align*}\] All normalizing constants cancel. Local updates and accepted/rejected swaps preserve the product target. Slots retain their temperatures; states move between slots. This is whole-target tempering, including the prior for a posterior target, rather than likelihood-only tempering.

In the notebook, inverse temperatures are \(1,0.5,0.2,0.08,0.025\); every replica starts in the left mode. Show the flattened densities, then the hot and cold traces. Ask how a state discovered in the right mode can reach the cold slot. The cold slot must visit both modes in the correct proportions, not merely alternate between them. Discard the initial burn-in and keep all retained repeats; never pool unweighted hot and cold samples. Compare methods at equal target-evaluation cost, since each sweep updates \(R\) replicas. Frequent swaps alone do not prove adequate exploration.

Why this swap rule?

Temperatures stay in their slots; the two states trade places

Slot \(r\), density \(\varrho_{\beta_r}\) Slot \(s\), density \(\varrho_{\beta_s}\)
Before \(x_r\) \(x_s\)
After \(x_s\) \(x_r\)

Compare the product density after with the product density before:

\[\begin{align*} R_{\mathrm{swap}} &=\frac{\text{density after}}{\text{density before}} =\frac{\varrho_{\beta_r}(x_s)\,\varrho_{\beta_s}(x_r)} {\varrho_{\beta_r}(x_r)\,\varrho_{\beta_s}(x_s)} =\left[\frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x_s)}{\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x_r)}\right]^{\beta_r-\beta_s} \end{align*}\]

  • Draw \(U\sim\Unif(0,1)\); swap if \(U\le\min\{1,R_{\mathrm{swap}}\}\)
  • Renaming \(r\) and \(s\): reciprocal density ratio and opposite exponent → same rule

Cold \(\beta_r=1\), hot \(\beta_s=1/2\), and \(\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x_s)=4\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x_r)\): \(R_{\mathrm{swap}}=\sqrt{4}=2\) → always swap; the reverse move is accepted with probability \(1/2\)

The ratio compares two configurations of the same pair of slots. It does not compare \(\varrho_{\beta_r}(x_s)\) with \(\varrho_{\beta_s}(x_r)\): those are the two factors in the numerator, not competing alternatives.

Write \(\varrho_{\beta}(x)=\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x)^\beta/C_\beta\). The constants \(C_{\beta_r}\) and \(C_{\beta_s}\) appear in both products and cancel. So do the densities for all unchanged slots in the full product target. The exponent matters: a hot density is proportional to a power of the target, not generally to the target itself.

For the example, take unnormalized target values 1 at \(x_r\) and 4 at \(x_s\). The before product is \(1^1\,4^{1/2}=2\); the after product is \(4^1\,1^{1/2}=4\). The ratio is \(4/2=2\). After that swap, proposing to move back gives \(2/4=1/2\). The accepted probability flows balance: \(2\times1=4\times(1/2)\), up to the common normalizing constant and proposal probability. Renaming slots changes neither physical configuration; reversing a move exchanges the before and after configurations. This distinction resolves the apparent asymmetry.

Symmetric positive definite kernels

A kernel \(K:\mathcal X\times\mathcal X\to\reals\) is symmetric positive definite when every Gram matrix

\[ \mK=(K(x_i,x_j))_{i,j=0}^{n-1} \]

is symmetric positive definite for distinct \(x_0,\ldots,x_{n-1}\)

Examples include

\[\begin{align*} K(t,x)&=\exp\!\left(-\norm{t-x}^2/h^2\right), &&\mathcal X=\reals^d,\\ K(t,x)&=\prod_{\ell=1}^d\left[1+\frac{\gamma_\ell^2}{2} \left(\abs{t_\ell-\tfrac12}+\abs{x_\ell-\tfrac12}-\abs{t_\ell-x_\ell}\right)\right], &&\mathcal X=[0,1]^d \end{align*}\]

  • First is a squared exponential kernel; second yields centered discrepancy

  • Kernels usually have parameters to be chosen or estimated

Discrepancy between distributions

Discrepancy
Measures

For independent \(X,X'\sim F_X\) and \(Z,Z'\sim F_Z\),

\[\begin{align*} D^2(F_X,F_Z;K) &=\Ex[K(X,X')]-2\Ex[K(X,Z)]+\Ex[K(Z,Z')]\\ &=\int_{\mathcal X^2}K(x,x')\,\dif F_X(x)\,\dif F_X(x') -2\int_{\mathcal X^2}K(x,x')\,\dif F_X(x)\,\dif F_Z(x')\\ &\qquad \qquad +\int_{\mathcal X^2}K(x,x')\,\dif F_Z(x)\,\dif F_Z(x')\\ &=\int_{\mathcal X^2}K(x,x')\,\dif(F_X-F_Z)(x)\,\dif(F_X-F_Z)(x') \ge0 \end{align*}\]

If both distributions have densities,

\[\begin{align*} D^2(F_X,F_Z;K) &=\int_{\mathcal X^2}K(x,x')\,\varrho_X(x)\,\varrho_X(x')\,\dif x\,\dif x' -2\int_{\mathcal X^2}K(x,x')\,\varrho_X(x)\,\varrho_Z(x')\,\dif x\,\dif x'\\ &\qquad \qquad +\int_{\mathcal X^2}K(x,x')\,\varrho_Z(x)\,\varrho_Z(x')\,\dif x\,\dif x'\\ &=\int_{\mathcal X^2}K(x,x') \{\varrho_X(x)-\varrho_Z(x)\} \{\varrho_X(x')-\varrho_Z(x')\}\,\dif x\,\dif x' \ge0 \end{align*}\]

Empirical distributions

Put mass \(1/n\) at each \(z_i\): \(\widehat F_{Z,n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\delta_{z_i}\)

Replace \(F_Z\) by \(\widehat F_{Z,n}\) in the distribution formula:

\[\begin{align*} D^2(F_X,\{z_i\}_{i=0}^{n-1};K) &=\int_{\mathcal X^2}K(x,x')\,\dif F_X(x)\,\dif F_X(x')\\ &\quad-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\int_{\mathcal X}K(x,z_i)\,\dif F_X(x)\\ &\quad+\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n-1}K(z_i,z_j) \end{align*}\]

Integration against an empirical distribution becomes averaging over its sample

Two empirical distributions

Replace both distributions by empirical distributions: integrals become sums

\[\begin{align*} D^2\!\left(\{x_i\}_{i=0}^{m-1},\{z_j\}_{j=0}^{n-1};K\right) &=\frac{1}{m^2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{m-1}K(x_i,x_j)\\ &\quad-\frac{2}{mn}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}K(x_i,z_j) +\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n-1}K(z_i,z_j) \end{align*}\]

  • The exact squared discrepancy of these empirical distributions; nonnegative
  • Direct computation requires \(\Order(\max(m,n)^2)\) kernel evaluations
  • The answer depends on the chosen kernel and its parameters

Interpreting sample discrepancy

Discrepancy
Measures
Statistics

Two questions, two quantities

 

Question Quantity
How different are these empirical distributions? Exact \(D^2(\widehat F_{X,m},\widehat F_{Z,n};K)\ge0\)
How different are the underlying distributions? Estimate \(D^2(F_X,F_Z;K)\) from random samples
  • The ordinary empirical formula answers the first question
  • As an estimator of population discrepancy, it is generally biased
  • With independent IID samples, removing diagonal terms gives an unbiased estimator
  • Unbiased estimates may be negative; the population squared discrepancy is not

Condition on the observed point sets for the first question: no unknown quantity remains in their empirical discrepancy. For the second question, regard the points as random draws from underlying distributions and ask about expectation over repeated samples. Reusing the same symbol without identifying these different targets can make the two formulas seem contradictory.

The off-diagonal correction uses independence within each sample as well as between the samples. Successive MCMC states do not satisfy the IID condition.

Unbiased estimates of squared discrepancy

For two independent IID samples, estimate \(D^2(F_X,F_Z;K)\)

Replace within-sample sums by off-diagonal averages:

\[\begin{align*} D_{\mathrm{unb}}^2\!\left(\{x_i\}_{i=0}^{m-1},\{z_j\}_{j=0}^{n-1};K\right) &=\frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{\substack{i,j=0\\i\ne j}}^{m-1}K(x_i,x_j)\\ &\quad-\frac{2}{mn}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}K(x_i,z_j) +\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{\substack{i,j=0\\i\ne j}}^{n-1}K(z_i,z_j) \end{align*}\]

Unbiased describes the estimator’s expectation, not the sign of each realization

A more negative realization is not a better match; it is a larger downward sampling fluctuation

What does unbiased mean?

\(\exstar\) Let \(\{X_i\}_{i=0}^{m-1}\) and \(\{Z_j\}_{j=0}^{n-1}\) be independent IID samples from \(F_X\) and \(F_Z\) Take expectations term by term to show \(\Ex[D_{\mathrm{unb}}^2]=D^2(F_X,F_Z;K)\) What is the expectation when \(F_X=F_Z\)? Why may a realization be negative, and does more negative mean a better match? Which step fails when the \(X_i\) are successive Metropolis states?

How small should discrepancy be?

Zero is the ideal; no universal acceptable cutoff

For a fixed target \(F_X\) and kernel \(K\), normalize by the no-sample error:

\[\begin{align*} D_0^2&=\int_{\mathcal X^2}K(x,x')\,\dif F_X(x)\,\dif F_X(x')>0, &\qquad D_{\mathrm{rel},n}^2&=\frac{D^2(F_X,\{z_i\}_{i=0}^{n-1};K)}{D_0^2} \end{align*}\]

  • \(D_{\mathrm{rel},n}^2<1\): improvement over estimating the integral by zero
  • Removes overall kernel amplitude; length scale and kernel shape still matter
  • No guaranteed \([0,1]\) range; compare samples using the same target and kernel
  • Study decay with \(n\): IID has \(\Ex[D_n^2]=C_K/n\), hence root mean squared discrepancy \(\Order(n^{-1/2})\)

Look for decreasing discrepancy and its asymptotic rate, together with the integration accuracy needed

The empty sample is not a probability distribution: \(D_0\) means the RKHS worst-case error of the zero quadrature rule, equivalently comparison with the zero measure. It is also the prior standard deviation of the integral under the zero-mean GP model. This normalization requires \(0<D_0^2<\infty\); a kernel centered with respect to the target can have \(D_0=0\) and cannot use this ratio.

Multiplying \(K\) by a positive constant multiplies both squared errors by that constant, so it cancels. Changing length scale changes which functions and spatial differences matter; normalization cannot remove that dependence. A relative squared discrepancy of \(0.01\) corresponds to a discrepancy of \(0.1\) times the no-sample error. It does not imply 1 percent relative error for every integrand. For a particular \(f\), use the bound \(D_n\norm{f}_{\mathcal H_K}\) and an application-specific tolerance.

Zero discrepancy means equal kernel mean embeddings; identifying equal probability distributions additionally requires a characteristic kernel. For IID sampling the later “What the kernel controls” slide gives the exact \(C_K/n\) expectation when the kernel moments are finite. This is a mean-square benchmark, not monotone decay on every realization. MCMC dependence changes the constant and may affect the rate without suitable mixing assumptions. A finite independent reference sample adds error: increase its size as well when studying decay, rather than interpreting a fixed-reference error floor as a failure to converge. Do not choose length scales simply to minimize the displayed discrepancy. The off-diagonal estimator can be negative and is not itself a squared norm.

A reproducing kernel Hilbert space

Discrepancy
Measures
Analysis

Every symmetric positive definite kernel \(K\) defines a Hilbert space \(\mathcal H_K\) of functions on \(\mathcal X\) with the reproducing property

\[ f(x)=\langle K(\cdot,x),f\rangle_{\mathcal H_K} \]

Assume \(\int_{\mathcal X^2}K(x,x')\,\dif F_X(x)\,\dif F_X(x')\) is finite

Then both

\[\begin{align*} f&\longmapsto\int_{\mathcal X}f(x)\,\dif F_X(x),\\ f&\longmapsto\int_{\mathcal X}f(x)\,\dif F_X(x)-\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(z_i) \end{align*}\]

are bounded linear functionals on \(\mathcal H_K\)

Worst-case integration error

Error
Assessment

By the Riesz representation theorem, some \(\zeta\in\mathcal H_K\) satisfies

\[ \int_{\mathcal X}f(x)\,\dif F_X(x)-\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(z_i) =\langle\zeta,f\rangle_{\mathcal H_K} \]

Therefore, by Cauchy–Schwarz,

\[ \left\lvert\int_{\mathcal X}f(x)\,\dif F_X(x)-\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(z_i)\right\rvert \le\norm{\zeta}_{\mathcal H_K}\norm{f}_{\mathcal H_K} \]

This separates sample quality, \(\norm{\zeta}_{\mathcal H_K}\), from integrand difficulty, \(\norm{f}_{\mathcal H_K}\)

The discrepancy identity

Error
Assessment

The representer is

\[ \zeta(x')=\int_{\mathcal X}K(x,x')\,\dif F_X(x) -\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}K(z_i,x') \]

Its squared norm is exactly the squared discrepancy:

\[\begin{align*} \norm{\zeta}_{\mathcal H_K}^2 &=\int_{\mathcal X^2}K(x,x')\,\dif F_X(x)\,\dif F_X(x')\\ &\quad-\frac2n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\int_{\mathcal X}K(x,z_i)\,\dif F_X(x) +\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n-1}K(z_i,z_j)\\ &=D^2(F_X,\{z_i\}_{i=0}^{n-1};K) \end{align*}\]

Thus discrepancy is the worst-case error over \(\norm{f}_{\mathcal H_K}\le1\)

Average-case integration error

Error
Assessment

Suppose \(f\) is modeled as a zero-mean Gaussian process with covariance kernel \(K\)

For deterministic \(z_0,\ldots,z_{n-1}\),

\[\begin{align*} &\Ex_f\!\left[ \left\{\int_{\mathcal X}f(x)\,\dif F_X(x)-\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(z_i)\right\}^2 \right]\\ &\quad=\int_{\mathcal X^2}K(x,x')\,\dif F_X(x)\,\dif F_X(x') -\frac2n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\int_{\mathcal X}K(x,z_i)\,\dif F_X(x) +\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n-1}K(z_i,z_j)\\ &\quad=D^2(F_X,\{z_i\}_{i=0}^{n-1};K) \end{align*}\]

Discrepancy is also the root mean squared error under the Gaussian-process model

Centered discrepancy and smoothness

Low
Discrepancy
Discrepancy

For \(\mathcal X=[0,1]^d\), the centered-discrepancy kernel is

\[ K(t,x)=\prod_{\ell=1}^d\left[1+\frac{\gamma_\ell^2}{2} \left(\abs{t_\ell-\tfrac12}+\abs{x_\ell-\tfrac12}-\abs{t_\ell-x_\ell}\right)\right] \]

Its norm combines the function value at the center with weighted square-integrals of mixed derivatives:

\[\begin{align*} \norm{f}_{\mathcal H_K}^2 &=\abs{f(1/2,\ldots,1/2)}^2 +\norm{\frac{\partial f(x_1,1/2,\ldots)}{\gamma_1\partial x_1}}_2^2\\ &\quad+\norm{\frac{\partial^2 f(x_1,x_2,1/2,\ldots)} {\gamma_1\gamma_2\partial x_1\partial x_2}}_2^2 +\cdots +\norm{\frac{\partial^d f(x)} {\gamma_1\cdots\gamma_d\partial x_1\cdots\partial x_d}}_2^2 \end{align*}\]

The weights \(\gamma_\ell\) express coordinate importance

Relative entropy

Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, also called relative entropy

For normalized densities \(\varrho_X\) and \(\varrho_Z\),

\[\begin{align*} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(F_X\Vert F_Z) &=\int_{\mathcal X}\varrho_X(x) \log\!\left(\frac{\varrho_X(x)}{\varrho_Z(x)}\right)\,\dif x \end{align*}\]

  • Nonnegative; zero exactly when the distributions agree
  • Generally asymmetric; infinite if \(F_X\) assigns mass where \(F_Z\) assigns none
  • Entropy describes one distribution; relative entropy compares two
  • Variational inference fits an approximate density by minimizing KL to the target

Finite empirical sample versus continuous target: KL is infinite

Kernel discrepancy compares these directly, without density estimation

Use natural logarithms and the convention that a zero numerator contributes zero. The density formula assumes a common dominating measure. More generally, KL is infinite unless the first distribution is absolutely continuous with respect to the second. A finite empirical measure and an atomless target are mutually singular, so both KL directions are infinite; density estimation or binning changes the comparison. This does not prevent studying KL convergence of a chain’s marginal law, which is distinct from its empirical measure.

Variational inference commonly minimizes \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(F_{\mathrm{approx}}\Vert F_{\mathrm{tar}})\) over a tractable family. It is an optimization alternative to MCMC, not itself an MCMC sampler. An unknown target normalizing constant contributes an additive constant to this objective; it does not prevent optimization, but is needed for its absolute numerical value.

References: Shalizi, Information Theory I, entropy and divergence; Blei, Kucukelbir, and McAuliffe, Variational Inference: A Review for Statisticians

KL minimization with an unnormalized target

With \(\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}=\widetilde\varrho/C\) and approximation density \(\varrho_{\mathrm{approx}}\),

\[\begin{align*} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(F_{\mathrm{approx}}\Vert F_{\mathrm{tar}}) &=\Ex_{\mathrm{approx}}\!\left[\log\!\left( \frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{approx}}(X)}{\widetilde\varrho(X)}\right)\right]+\log C \end{align*}\]

Fixed target: \(\log C\) does not affect minimization over the approximation

The KL optimization statement is for a fixed target and the displayed direction. It is not permission to omit a parameter-dependent normalizer when fitting the target model. Density or density-ratio estimators can estimate KL from samples, but that differs from plugging an atomic empirical measure into KL.

Stein discrepancy

For a smooth positive target density \(\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x)=\widetilde\varrho(x)/C\), the score removes the unknown normalizing constant

\[\begin{align*} s(x)&=\nabla_x\log\varrho_{\mathrm{tar}}(x) =\nabla_x\log\widetilde\varrho(x)\\ \mathcal T g(x)&=s(x)^\mathsf{T}g(x)+\nabla\!\cdot g(x), \qquad g:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R^d \end{align*}\]

Stein’s identity: \(\Ex_{\mathrm{tar}}[\mathcal T g(X)]=0\) for suitable \(g\)

For candidate points \(z_0,\ldots,z_{n-1}\), write the error as true value minus sample average:

\[\begin{align*} D_{\mathrm{Stein}}(F_{\mathrm{tar}},\{z_i\};\mathcal G) &=\sup_{g\in\mathcal G}\left| \underbrace{\Ex_{\mathrm{tar}}[\mathcal T g(X)]}_{0} -\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathcal T g(z_i)\right| \end{align*}\]

The largest departure from zero over a size-constrained class \(\mathcal G\) of test functions

This slide describes the common Langevin, score-based Stein operator for a continuous target, not every Stein discrepancy. Here \(g\) is a vector field; the identity follows by integration by parts when the boundary term vanishes and the expectations exist. On bounded supports, the function class or operator must enforce the appropriate boundary conditions. Discrete targets require a different operator.

For candidate points \(x_0,\ldots,x_{n-1}\), the discrepancy is \(\sup_{g\in\mathcal G}|0-n^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathcal T g(x_i)|\), with the true target integral first and the sample approximation second. Applying Stein operators to both arguments of a sufficiently smooth base kernel gives a target-dependent Stein kernel and a pairwise-sum formula for KSD squared. Thus KSD is closely related to MMD, using a specially constructed kernel whose target expectation vanishes. It is not a wholly unrelated alternative to kernel discrepancy. Direct pairwise computation is quadratic in sample size.

Suitable kernels and target assumptions give convergence-determining KSDs; the failure of some kernels is not a universal failure of Stein discrepancy. Weak sensitivity to weights of well-separated modes is a finite-sample testing limitation, distinct from an asymptotic convergence theorem.

References: Liu, Lee, and Jordan (2016); Gorham and Mackey (2017); Liu, Duncan, and Gandy (2023)

Computing kernel Stein discrepancy

For kernel Stein discrepancy (KSD), choose \(\mathcal G\) as the unit ball of \(\mathcal H_K^d\)

The supremum has an explicit formula using a Stein kernel:

\[\begin{align*} K_{\mathrm{Stein}}(x,z) &=s(x)^\mathsf{T}s(z)K(x,z) +s(x)^\mathsf{T}\nabla_z K(x,z)\\ &\quad+s(z)^\mathsf{T}\nabla_x K(x,z) +\sum_{\ell=1}^d\frac{\partial^2 K(x,z)}{\partial x_\ell\,\partial z_\ell}\\[0.5ex] \mathrm{KSD}^2(F_{\mathrm{tar}},\{z_i\};K) &=\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n-1}K_{\mathrm{Stein}}(z_i,z_j)\ge0 \end{align*}\]

  • The target-integral terms vanish: only the sample-pair sum remains
  • Needs target scores and kernel derivatives; no target draws or normalizing constant

Requires smoothness and valid boundary conditions; kernel choice matters

Some KSDs fail to detect nonconvergence; separated-mode weights can be hard to assess

Here \(\mathcal H_K^d\) is the space of vector fields whose components belong to the RKHS of the scalar base kernel \(K\), with squared norm equal to the sum of the component squared norms. Its unit ball prevents arbitrary rescaling of \(g\). The smoothness, integrability, and boundary assumptions must allow the Stein identity to hold for the relevant kernel sections and derivatives. Then \(\Ex_{X\sim F_{\mathrm{tar}}}[K_{\mathrm{Stein}}(X,z)]=0\), so the target-target and target-sample terms in ordinary squared discrepancy are zero. The displayed sum includes the diagonal: it is the exact squared KSD of the empirical measure, not the off-diagonal unbiased estimator of a population quantity. No independence assumption is needed for this finite-point formula, though dependence matters for its sampling behavior.

Reference: Liu, Lee, and Jordan (2016), Theorem 3.6

Choose by access to the distribution and which details affect the answer

The finite horizon makes a finance path a finite collection of random inputs after discretization; it does not by itself make direct sampling easy. In the models used here, Gaussian increments or known transition laws are available, so independent inputs generate independent paths, even though values within each path are dependent and may form a Markov process. Low discrepancy inputs are another choice, but do not give independent paths.

For geometric Brownian motion, transitions on a chosen grid can be sampled exactly; a European terminal payoff needs no intervening time steps. A continuously averaged Asian payoff still needs control of path-integration error. A barrier payoff can depend critically on crossings between grid points. Refinement and Brownian-bridge corrections address different sources of error. Multilevel Monte Carlo combines many cheap coarse paths with fewer coupled fine-minus-coarse corrections; it is not MCMC.

A sharp posterior alone does not require MCMC: a narrow normal or a conjugate posterior may be sampled directly. The obstacle is the lack of an effective direct sampler for the posterior geometry, often with only an unnormalized density available. Narrow directions and separated modes can also impede MCMC.

Queueing belongs here as simulation of a Markov system, not as a claim that Metropolis–Hastings is needed for queues. Event order and state-dependent interactions determine waiting and blocking. Simulate directly from arrival and service laws, advancing to the next event. Both finite-horizon and steady-state questions are possible; steady-state time averages require appropriate time weighting and attention to initialization.

Additional spoken examples: reliability simulation with directly sampled component lifetimes; inventory systems with event-driven demand and restocking.

Our Monte Carlo error assessments so far have used a frequentist perspective

  • Frequentist: assess estimators by repeated-sampling bias, variance, and coverage; no likelihood is required
  • Fisherian / likelihood: compare parameter values through how well they explain the observed data
  • Bayesian: combine a sampling model with a prior to describe posterior uncertainty

These perspectives overlap: MLE can be assessed frequentist-wise, and Bayesian inference also uses likelihood

B. Efron & T. Hastie, Computer Age Statistical Inference (2016), Chapters 2–4

Efron and Hastie distinguish frequentist, Fisherian, and Bayesian traditions in B. Efron & T. Hastie, Computer Age Statistical Inference (2016), Chapters 2–4. The bullets emphasize the aspects relevant to this course, rather than giving an exhaustive account of each tradition.

Our sample means, standard errors, and confidence intervals have been evaluated through their behavior under repeated sampling. This does not require specifying or maximizing a parametric likelihood. For example, a central limit theorem can justify an approximate confidence interval without assuming the observations themselves are normal.

We now introduce likelihood to explain the ingredients of Bayesian inference. Maximizing it is one possible use; multiplying it by a prior is another. An MLE is not a prerequisite for Bayes.

Likelihood and maximum likelihood estimation

The likelihood is the shared starting point for MLE and Bayesian inference

For observed data \(y\) and parameter \(\theta\),

\[ L(\theta;y)=\varrho_{Y\mid\theta}(y\mid\theta) \]

  • Use the sampling model’s density or probability mass function; no normality assumption
  • Hold \(y\) fixed and compare candidate parameter values \(\theta\)

The maximum likelihood estimator selects a maximizer:

\[ \htheta_{\mathrm{MLE}}\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\theta}L(\theta;y) \]

MLE: maximize the likelihood for a point estimate
Bayesian inference: combine the same likelihood with a prior for a posterior distribution

MLE is an alternative method of inference, not a required step before Bayes. The likelihood is the precursor common to both. Frequentist inference can add confidence intervals and other uncertainty assessments; it is not limited to point estimation. Bayesian inference can also report point summaries.

The normal model below is one worked example, not an assumption of the general likelihood definition. The Bayesian notebook uses a normal observation model with known variance to make an exact posterior benchmark available.

For \(Y_0,\ldots,Y_{n-1}\ \IIDsim\ \Norm(\mu,\sigma^2)\),

\[\begin{align*} L(\mu,\sigma^2;y) &=(2\mpi\sigma^2)^{-n/2} \exp\!\left[-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(y_i-\mu)^2\right],\\ \hmu_{\mathrm{MLE}}&=\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}y_i,& \hsigma^2_{\mathrm{MLE}}&=\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(y_i-\hmu_{\mathrm{MLE}})^2 \end{align*}\]

Consider a single-server queue observed at event times

\[\begin{align*} A_i&=\text{time until the next arrival},\\ N_i&=\text{number of customers in the system},\\ S_i&=\text{remaining service time} \end{align*}\]

Draw full interarrival times from \(F_A\) and full service times from \(F_S\)

Residual times generally have different laws

The state \((A_i,N_i,S_i)\) is Markov when new interarrival and service durations are independent

At each event, either

  • the next customer arrives before service finishes, or
  • the current service finishes before the next arrival

Event-driven queue simulation

Initialize A[0] ~ F_A, N[0] = 0, S[0] = infinity
while the stopping rule is not met:
    if N[i] == 0:
        advance to the next arrival
        N[i+1] = 1
        generate S[i+1] ~ F_S and A[i+1] ~ F_A
    elif A[i] < S[i]:
        advance to the next arrival
        N[i+1] = N[i] + 1
        S[i+1] = S[i] - A[i]
        generate A[i+1] ~ F_A
    else:
        advance to the next service completion
        N[i+1] = N[i] - 1
        A[i+1] = A[i] - S[i]
        if N[i+1] > 0: generate S[i+1] ~ F_S
        else: S[i+1] = infinity

Events replace fixed time steps, avoiding work when nothing changes

At an arrival after elapsed time \(\Delta t\):

  • add \(\Delta t\) to every customer currently in the system
  • initialize the arriving customer’s clock

At a service completion:

  • record the departing customer’s \(W_j\)
