Parallel computing

Practice

Modern CPUs and GPUs can evaluate many operations concurrently

  • Monte Carlo function values are often pleasingly parallel: samples can be evaluated independently
  • Parallel random-number streams must remain nonoverlapping and statistically sound
  • GPU arithmetic is often optimized for 32-bit floating point rather than 64-bit floating point
  • Reduced precision may lose significant digits but can increase throughput substantially
  • GPUs help most when there are many similar operations
    Launch and data-transfer costs can dominate small problems

Parallelism changes computational cost

It does not repair biased estimators or poor sampling designs

Faster computation and statistical accuracy

Compare a baseline and a faster implementation: single-core / multicore CPU, or CPU / GPU
For fixed sampling method; runtime proportional to sample count

\[\begin{equation*} \left. \begin{aligned} s=\frac{\operatorname{time}_{\mathrm{baseline}}(n)}{\operatorname{time}_{\mathrm{faster}}(n)}>1 &\quad\implies\quad n_{\mathrm{faster}}\approx s\,n_{\mathrm{baseline}}\\ \operatorname{error}(n)\approx A n^{-p} &\quad\implies\quad \frac{\operatorname{error}(n_{\mathrm{faster}})}{\operatorname{error}(n_{\mathrm{baseline}})}\approx\frac1{s^p} \end{aligned} \right\} \quad\begin{gathered}\alerttext{for the same}\\\alerttext{time budget}\end{gathered} \end{equation*}\]

Error: RMSE for IID/RQMC; absolute-error bound for QMC

Method Power \(p\) Conditions
IID \(1/2\) Finite contribution variance
QMC \(\approx1\) Sufficient smoothness and a suitable design
RQMC \(\approx3/2\) Sufficient smoothness and suitable scrambling

Speedup \(s=16\): about \(1/4\) of the IID RMSE, \(1/16\) of the QMC bound, \(1/64\) of the RQMC RMSE

Assume negligible floating-point error and comparable variance, and ignore setup/overhead in the same-time calculation. Float32 can eventually impose a numerical error floor; the saved experiment does not locate that floor. time_baseline(n)/time_faster(n) compares the time for the same n contributions. In the throughput regime, time is approximately proportional to sample count, so speedup s permits s times as many points at the same time budget. For a model error(n) approximately A n^{-p}, the ratio is s^{-p}. Keep the sampling method, integrand, and error constant A comparable between implementations. IID uses RMSE sigma/sqrt(n); deterministic QMC uses an absolute-error bound, not RMSE over randomizations or a ratio of actual errors. The displayed QMC/RQMC powers suppress logarithmic factors and are illustrative, not universal guarantees. QMC requires the smoothness/variation assumptions of its bound and a suitable point design. Smooth-integrand RQMC rates near n^{-3/2} require an appropriate randomization, such as nested uniform scrambling of suitable digital nets; a generic random shift does not ensure that rate. For replicated RQMC, keep the number of replications fixed. The s=16 examples use the nominal powers 1/2, 1, and 3/2. This applies to single-core versus multicore CPU implementations as well as CPU versus GPU implementations; name the actual baseline in either case. Total sample count controls sampling error; batch size controls memory and throughput. At fixed batch size, speedup can level off as both runtimes become proportional to n. Eight replications give a noisy empirical RMSE; a lucky point does not rank precision. In the M3 fixed-n sweep, changing batches from 4096 to 262144 reduces GPU runtime from 34.22 to 3.92 ms without changing n=1048576 or its IID error law. Larger batches need not always help; choose using measurements and memory limits.

MLMC glossary: samples and work

Symbol Meaning
\(n_\ell\), \(n=\sum_\ell n_\ell\) Level sample count and total sample count
\(\$_\ell\) Work for one complete contribution at level \(\ell\)
\(w_\ell\) Sample allocation weight: \(n_\ell/n=w_\ell/\sum_k w_k\)

Total work, for two or many levels

\[ C=n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2+\cdots+n_L\$_L =\sum_{\ell=1}^L n_\ell\$_\ell \]

\(C(\varepsilon)\) is the work needed at error tolerance \(\varepsilon\)

Sample counts and work shares differ

Introduce the method-specific amplitude A_l and decay power p only when needed in the two-level error models. For IID, A_l^2=V_l. Random methods use RMSE; deterministic QMC uses a justified absolute-error bound.

The fixed work budget C determines n; n and C are not independently imposed budgets. Work shares are proportional to cost_l w_l, whereas sample shares are proportional to w_l. Complete contribution work includes inputs, transforms, and coarse/fine evaluations with any legitimate reuse. Setup, pilots, and allowed sample-size rounding must also be charged.

For RQMC, n_l=R N_l counts all evaluations over a fixed number R of independent randomizations with N_l points in each run. Two-level Monte Carlo

Estimation

We want to compute \(\Ex\bigl[f_{d}(\vX_{1:d})\bigr]\) for large \(d\)

But large \(d\) costs more, e.g., \(\$\bigl(f_d(\vX_{1:d})\bigr)\propto d\)

  • Computational finance: \(d=\) time steps \(\times\) number of assets
  • ODE/PDE models with random fields: \(d=\) grid points representing the field

Choose \(d_1\ll d_2 = d\) and couple the coarse and fine approximations

\[\begin{align*} Y_1&=f_{d_1}(\vX_{1:d_1})& &\alerttext{cheap}\text{ approximation}\\ Y_2&=f_{d_2}(\vX_{1:d_2})-f_{d_1}(\vX_{1:d_1})& &\alerttext{expensive}\text{ correction} \end{align*}\]

\[ \mu=\Ex\{f_{d}(\vX_{1:d})\}=\Ex(Y_1)+\Ex(Y_2) \]

Estimate the cheap part often and the expensive correction less often, if its error is easier to control

Here mu is the full two-level mean at the fixed fine resolution d=d2, equal to mu1+mu2. The hierarchy section separately distinguishes a finite fine mean from a limiting target when introducing approximation bias. The displayed linear cost is an illustrative model for evaluation, not a universal dimension law. Cost includes input generation, transforms, and evaluation; dense factorizations or other solvers can cost more than O(d). In finance, d can count the scalar random inputs for a time-by-asset path discretization. In a random-field ODE/PDE model, d can count field values on a grid; the field-generation and solver costs need not be linear in d. A complete coupled correction costs roughly the fine evaluation plus any coarse work that cannot be reused. Its cost is typically of order d2 in this illustrative setting, whereas the cheap level costs order d1. Within Y2, coarse and fine approximations share a path or compatible inputs. For time discretizations, preserve Brownian values at shared times; a Brownian bridge can refine the coarse path. Between the two level estimators, use independent samples or independent RQMC randomizations. A small mean correction does not imply a small variance or a small QMC error constant. Coupling should make the correction easier to integrate; that property, together with the cost ratio, must justify two levels.

Coupling coarse and fine Brownian paths

Choose nested monitoring grids on \([0,T]\) with \(d_2=\kappa d_1\), \(\kappa>1\)

  • Order the Brownian values \(\vX\) with the coarse-grid values first
  • Use a Brownian bridge to add values at the extra fine-grid times

For an option, \(f_{d_\ell}(\vX_{1:d_\ell})\) computes its discounted payoff
using the path values at \(d_\ell\) monitoring times

\[\begin{align*} Y_1&=f_{d_1}(\vX_{1:d_1})&&\alerttext{cheap}\text{ coarse payoff}\\ Y_2&=f_{d_2}(\vX_{1:d_2})-f_{d_1}(\vX_{1:d_1})&&\alerttext{expensive}\text{ payoff correction} \end{align*}\]

  • Estimate \(\Ex(Y_1)\) from coarse-only paths
  • Estimate \(\Ex(Y_2)\) from a separate independent set of bridge-refined paths

The two payoffs in \(Y_2\) use the same Brownian path at two resolutions

Here X_1:d1 contains coarse-grid Brownian values. Append the conditionally generated Brownian-bridge values at added times to obtain X_1:d2. This is hierarchical rather than chronological ordering. Each payoff function knows the monitoring times attached to its inputs and restores chronological order when needed. Thus the coarse vector is literally a prefix of the fine vector, matching the f_d notation on the preceding slide. Nested grids may be nonuniform. Counts d1 and d2 are positive integers and kappa=d2/d1>1. Equally spaced nested grids require an integer refinement factor; general nested grids allow other ratios. No bridge-generation details are needed here. The notebook’s dyadic grids illustrate one particular choice. The fine and coarse approximations use the same Brownian values at shared times; their payoffs need not agree. Generate correction paths independently of the coarse-only sample set used for the cheap mean. For RQMC, use independent randomizations between level estimates; points within a run may be dependent. The coarse-only sample has the same marginal law as the coarse part of a refined path, preserving the telescoping expectation identity. Coupling’s benefit still depends on the correction’s method-specific error.

Random two-level estimates: IID or RQMC

Estimate \(\mu_\ell=\Ex(Y_\ell)\) with uncorrelated level sample means: \(\operatorname{cov}(\hmu_1,\hmu_2)=0\)
Independent sample sets \(\{Y_{i,1}\}_{i=0}^{n_1-1}\) and \(\{Y_{i,2}\}_{i=0}^{n_2-1}\) ensure this condition

\[\begin{align*} \hmu&=\hmu_1+\hmu_2& \hmu_\ell&=\frac1{n_\ell}\sum_{i=0}^{n_\ell-1}Y_{i,\ell}\\ \var(\hmu)&=\var(\hmu_1)+\var(\hmu_2)& C&=n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2 \end{align*}\]

For unbiased level estimates, model \(\operatorname{RMSE}(\hmu_\ell)\approx A_\ell n_\ell^{-p}\)

\[ \operatorname{MSE}(\hmu) =\var(\hmu) \approx\frac{A_1^2}{n_1^{2p}}+\frac{A_2^2}{n_2^{2p}} \]

  • IID: \(p=1/2\), \(A_\ell^2=\var(Y_\ell)\); the variance model is exact
  • RQMC: \(p\) and \(A_\ell\) depend on smoothness, design, and randomization
    Points within each sample set may be dependent

The MSE here is relative to the full two-level mean mu at the fixed fine resolution. Use a positive common exponent p for this allocation model and positive amplitudes A_l. Different levels can have different rates; the common-p formulas are a simplifying model, not a claim that all rates agree. For RQMC, Y_il are values from a randomized design with the correct marginals. Unbiasedness is assumed, and the variance is over design randomizations. Variance addition requires Cov(hmu1,hmu2)=0; independence is sufficient, not necessary. The explicitly indexed sample sets can be generated independently between levels, with independent RQMC randomizations. Within-level points may be dependent. The algebraically defined Y1 and Y2 need not be uncorrelated when evaluated on the same path; the zero-covariance condition is on their separately sampled level means. Within the correction Y2, retain the coarse/fine coupling. With a fixed number R of independent runs per level, n_l can denote total evaluations; absorb fixed replication factors into A_l and keep cost per evaluation consistent. More explicitly, n_l=R N_l and Var(mean of R runs) approximately a_l^2 N_l^{-2p}/R, so the amplitude in total-count notation is A_l=a_l R^{p-1/2}. Keep R fixed when varying n_l. Smooth integrands under suitable scrambling may have p near 3/2, ignoring logarithmic factors; a generic random shift does not guarantee that rate.

Allocating work optimally

Estimation

For independent unbiased random estimates, minimize the MSE model

\[ \frac{A_1^2}{n_1^{2p}}+\frac{A_2^2}{n_2^{2p}} \quad\text{subject to}\quad n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2=C \]

The optimal continuous allocation satisfies

\[ \frac{n_1}{n_2} =\left(\frac{A_1^2\$_2}{A_2^2\$_1}\right)^{1/(2p+1)} \]

IID (\(p=1/2\), \(A_\ell^2=V_\ell\)): sample allocation weights

\[ w_\ell=\frac{A_\ell}{\sqrt{\$_\ell}}=\sqrt{\frac{V_\ell}{\$_\ell}} \qquad\frac{n_1}{n_2}=\frac{w_1}{w_2} \qquad\frac{n_\ell}{n}=\frac{w_\ell}{w_1+w_2} \]

More points for larger error amplitude; fewer for larger cost per point

For positive amplitudes and costs, differentiate the Lagrangian: \(2p A_\ell^2 n_\ell^{-(2p+1)}=\lambda\$_\ell\). The common factor \(2p\) cancels in the ratio. This is exact optimization of the continuous power-law model; RQMC power laws are generally approximations. At p=1/2 the optimal IID MSE is \((\sqrt{V_1\$_1}+\sqrt{V_2\$_2})^2/C\), as in the original IID treatment. Round to positive admissible sample sizes; RQMC may require powers of a base. Include setup and pilot costs in practical work. Pilot estimates of IID variance do not automatically identify RQMC amplitudes or rates; use whole randomized estimates and justify the applicable model.

Deriving the two-level allocation

For the random case, treat \(n_1,n_2>0\) as continuous; fix total work \(C\)

\[ \underbrace{\mathcal L}_{\text{Lagrangian}} =\frac{A_1^2}{n_1^{2p}}+\frac{A_2^2}{n_2^{2p}} +\lambda(n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2-C) \]

\[\begin{align*} \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial n_\ell}=0 &\implies\frac{2pA_\ell^2}{n_\ell^{2p+1}}=\lambda\$_\ell \qquad(\ell=1,2)\\ \frac{A_1^2}{\$_1n_1^{2p+1}} &=\frac{A_2^2}{\$_2n_2^{2p+1}} \implies\frac{n_1}{n_2} =\left(\frac{A_1^2\$_2}{A_2^2\$_1}\right)^{1/(2p+1)} \end{align*}\]

The sample allocation weights give

\[ w_\ell=\left(\frac{A_\ell^2}{\$_\ell}\right)^{1/(2p+1)} \qquad n_\ell=\frac{Cw_\ell}{\$_1w_1+\$_2w_2} =\frac{nw_\ell}{w_1+w_2} \]

At the optimum, each level buys the same marginal MSE reduction per unit work

This derives the random allocation on the preceding slide. Keep the IID marginal-MSE continuations with this derivation before the deterministic bounds. Differentiate the MSE model, not RMSE, since their minimizers agree. The magnitude of the derivative with respect to work on level ell is 2p A_ell^2/(cost_ell n_ell^(2p+1)). If these marginal gains differ, transfer a little work toward the level with the larger gain. For p>0 and positive amplitudes and costs, the objective is strictly convex and diverges at either boundary of the feasible budget segment; the stationary point is the unique global continuous minimum. RQMC still uses an approximate variance model. Round up to allowed counts when targeting a tolerance; the fixed-budget continuous solution may need adjustment for admissible sizes.

IID: MSE decrease per unit work

Treat \(n_\ell>0\) as continuous; keep \(A_\ell,\$_\ell\) fixed

\[\begin{align*} \operatorname{MSE}_\ell&=\frac{A_\ell^2}{n_\ell} &\implies\quad \frac{\mathrm d\operatorname{MSE}_\ell}{\mathrm dn_\ell} &=-\frac{A_\ell^2}{n_\ell^2}\\ \operatorname{cost}_\ell&=n_\ell\$_\ell &\implies\quad \frac{\mathrm d\operatorname{cost}_\ell}{\mathrm dn_\ell}&=\$_\ell \end{align*}\]

The MSE decrease per unit work is the ratio of these derivatives

\[ -\frac{\mathrm d\operatorname{MSE}_\ell}{\mathrm d\operatorname{cost}_\ell} =-\frac{\mathrm d\operatorname{MSE}_\ell/\mathrm dn_\ell} {\mathrm d\operatorname{cost}_\ell/\mathrm dn_\ell} =\frac{A_\ell^2}{\$_\ell n_\ell^2} \]

The derivative is evaluated in the continuous allocation model, with A_ell and cost per contribution fixed and positive. The minus sign makes the MSE decrease positive. The chain rule converts a change per extra sample into a change per unit of work. If the two derivative magnitudes differ, transfer a small amount of work toward the level with the larger magnitude; the total MSE decreases to first order. The Lagrange equations on the preceding slide set these magnitudes equal to lambda. For IID, w_ell is A_ell/sqrt(cost per contribution), so equal magnitudes imply equal w_ell/n_ell. Setup, pilots, and sample-size rounding remain outside this linear model. The next continuation connects the derivative to level RMSE and sample weights.

IID: interpreting the optimal allocation

Write \(\operatorname{err}_\ell^2=\operatorname{MSE}_\ell\) and \(w_\ell=A_\ell/\sqrt{\$_\ell}\)

\[ -\frac{\mathrm d\operatorname{MSE}_\ell}{\mathrm d\operatorname{cost}_\ell} =\frac{A_\ell^2}{\$_\ell n_\ell^2} =\frac{\operatorname{err}_\ell^2}{\operatorname{cost}_\ell} =\left(\frac{w_\ell}{n_\ell}\right)^2 \]

If one level has a larger value, moving a little work to it lowers total MSE

At the optimum, both derivative magnitudes equal \(\lambda\)

\[ \left(\frac{w_1}{n_1}\right)^2 =\left(\frac{w_2}{n_2}\right)^2=\lambda \implies\frac{n_1}{n_2}=\frac{w_1}{w_2} \]

Optimal allocation equalizes the MSE decrease per unit work

The term err_ell is the RMSE of the level sample mean, not an observed error. This interpretation uses the continuous IID model on the preceding slide. Positive weights and sample counts let us take square roots of the equality.

Deterministic two-level estimates

Use QMC separately for the same cheap part and coupled correction

Assume justified absolute-error bounds with a common \(p>0\)

\[\begin{align*} \abs{\hmu_\ell-\mu_\ell}&\le\frac{A_\ell}{n_\ell^p}\\ \abs{\hmu-\mu} &\le\abs{\hmu_1-\mu_1}+\abs{\hmu_2-\mu_2} \le\frac{A_1}{n_1^p}+\frac{A_2}{n_2^p} \end{align*}\]

At fixed work \(n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2=C\), minimize the bound

\[ w_\ell=\left(\frac{A_\ell}{\$_\ell}\right)^{1/(p+1)} \qquad\frac{n_1}{n_2}=\frac{w_1}{w_2} =\left(\frac{A_1\$_2}{A_2\$_1}\right)^{1/(p+1)} \]

QMC needs sufficient smoothness and a suitable design
Bound total error by the sum of the level error bounds

This is a triangle-inequality upper bound, not a sum-of-variances identity or an equality for the realized integration error. No independence or unbiasedness is asserted for deterministic estimates. Minimize the stated upper bound, not the unknown actual error. Differentiation gives \(p A_\ell n_\ell^{-(p+1)}=\lambda\$_\ell\) and the displayed ratio. A_l here is a deterministic error-bound amplitude, not the RQMC amplitude or the contribution SD. For a suitable RKHS bound it may be a discrepancy rate constant times the variation of the complete level integrand. An illustrative rate is p near 1 with appropriate smoothness and design; retain logarithmic factors if needed. A small correction variance alone does not guarantee a small deterministic QMC error amplitude. This bound is relative to the full two-level mean mu at the fixed fine resolution. Limiting-target approximation bias is treated separately in the hierarchy section.

What two levels accomplish

  • \(d_1\ll d_2\): the coarse approximation can be much cheaper
  • Coupling can make the expensive correction easier to integrate
  • Allocate points using the error amplitudes, rates, and complete evaluation costs
  • IID/RQMC: independent unbiased level estimates add variances
  • Deterministic QMC: level error bounds add by an inequality
  • Fix the target dimension \(d_2\); increase \(n_1,n_2\) to control integration error

Two levels help when the cheap approximation and small correction error earn their combined cost

The correction must be small in the sense relevant to the integration method: variance for IID, randomized-estimate error amplitude for RQMC, and a justified variation/discrepancy bound for deterministic QMC. Neither a small correction mean nor pointwise closeness alone establishes all these properties.

Two-level random cost as a function of error

Fix grids, amplitudes, and costs; use the IID or RQMC model with common \(p>0\)

Scale the optimal counts together: \(n_1=t w_1\), \(n_2=t w_2\), \(t>0\)

From the optimal-weight definition: \(A_1^2=\$_1w_1^{2p+1}\) and \(A_2^2=\$_2w_2^{2p+1}\)

\[\begin{align*} S&=\$_1w_1+\$_2w_2 \qquad C=n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2=tS\\ \operatorname{MSE}(\hmu) &\approx\frac{A_1^2}{(tw_1)^{2p}}+\frac{A_2^2}{(tw_2)^{2p}}\\ &=\frac{\$_1w_1^{2p+1}}{t^{2p}w_1^{2p}} +\frac{\$_2w_2^{2p+1}}{t^{2p}w_2^{2p}} =\frac{\$_1w_1+\$_2w_2}{t^{2p}}=\frac{S}{t^{2p}} \end{align*}\]

\[ \operatorname{RMSE}(\hmu)\approx\frac{\sqrt S}{t^p}\approx\varepsilon \implies t\approx\left(\frac{\sqrt S}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/p} \]

\[ C(\varepsilon)=tS \approx S\left(\frac{\sqrt S}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/p} =S^{1+1/(2p)}\varepsilon^{-1/p} \]

Use unbiased, uncorrelated level estimates and the same positive sampling-error power p on both levels. The optimal weights fix the ratio of the two sample counts; the single scale t controls their size and the total work. The weight definitions give A12=cost1*w1(2p+1) and A22=cost2*w2(2p+1), making the cancellation explicit for both levels. Set the modeled RMSE equal to the requested tolerance to find the required scale, then multiply by S to obtain total work. The general random model is approximate. For IID, p=1/2, A1^2=V1, and A2^2=V2, so it gives exactly C(epsilon)=(sqrt(V1cost1)+sqrt(V2cost2))2/epsilon2 in the continuous model; the next slide repeats this IID formula for the direct-fine comparison. When does two-level IID save work?

Compare estimates of the same fine target \(\mu\) at RMSE \(\varepsilon\)

Direct fine contributions have variance \(V\) and complete per-sample work \(\$\)

\[ C_{\mathrm{single}}(\varepsilon)=\frac{V\$}{\varepsilon^2} \qquad C_{\mathrm{two}}(\varepsilon) =\frac{\left(\sqrt{V_1\$_1}+\sqrt{V_2\$_2}\right)^2}{\varepsilon^2} \]

With optimal two-level allocation, we save work precisely when

\[ \left(\sqrt{V_1\$_1}+\sqrt{V_2\$_2}\right)^2<V\$ \]

The same \(\varepsilon^{-2}\) factor cancels; compare the cost constants

V is the variance of one direct evaluation of f_d2(X_1:d2), and dollar is the work for generating its inputs, transforming them, and evaluating that fine payoff. It need not equal dollar_2: a correction also evaluates the coarse payoff, allowing reuse. Both strategies target the same finite mean, so no approximation bias enters this RMSE comparison. The formulas are not based on V=V1+V2: the shared-path coarse payoff and correction may be correlated. Independence is imposed between the separately sampled level means. The formulas are exact in the continuous IID allocation model with independent level means; they omit setup, pilots, integer rounding, and minimum sample counts. Such additional costs must be included in a practical savings comparison.

Why the two-level cost constant can be smaller

On one shared path, the fine payoff is \(Y_1+Y_2\), so

\[ V=\var(Y_1+Y_2)=V_1+V_2+2\sqrt{V_1V_2}\corr(Y_1,Y_2) \]

  • If coupling makes \(V_2\ll V_1\), then \(V\approx V_1\)
  • If the extra coarse work is small, then \(\$_2\approx\$\)

Under these approximations,

\[ \frac{C_{\mathrm{two}}(\varepsilon)}{C_{\mathrm{single}}(\varepsilon)} \approx \left(\sqrt{\frac{\$_1}{\$_2}}+\sqrt{\frac{V_2}{V_1}}\right)^2 \]

Cheap coarse work and small correction variance can make this ratio less than one

The correlation concerns payoffs on one shared path; level sample means are sampled independently. The correlation is bounded between -1 and 1, so V1 is the leading term when V2 is much smaller than V1. The correction costs roughly the fine contribution plus extra coarse work that cannot be reused; when that extra work is small, dollar_2 is close to the direct fine cost. Substituting V approximately V1 and dollar approximately dollar_2 into the exact cost ratio gives the displayed expression. These are conditional approximations in the continuous IID work model, not guaranteed savings for every hierarchy. Setup, pilots, and rounding still need to be counted in an implemented comparison.

If R runs with N_l points per run are averaged, the across-run sample variance divided by R estimates the variance of the reported mean, and the total point count in the displayed convention is n_l=R N_l. Keep R fixed when comparing point counts and in the A_l model. Under an approximate power law, at a fixed level compare total counts n_ell and a larger allowed r n_ell, giving \(\hat p_\ell=\log[\hat v_\ell(n_\ell)/\hat v_\ell(rn_\ell)]/(2\log r)\). Several counts and replications are preferable to treating one noisy ratio as an established rate. If levels have different rates, do not force the common-p closed form: use the level-specific multiplier equations or a measured marginal variance-reduction-per-work allocation. Timing needs the complete coupled contribution and a representative batch; separate fixed setup from per-point cost and include GPU synchronization. Freeze allocation after an independent pilot for a simple final analysis. Adaptive reuse needs its own unbiasedness and uncertainty justification. Source: Giles, Multilevel Monte Carlo methods (2015).

Validate the remaining error budget

  • Deterministic QMC: obtain \(A_\ell,p\) from justified smoothness, discrepancy, or coefficient-tail bounds
    Differences between point counts are diagnostics; they do not by themselves certify an error bound
  • Approximation bias: choose \(L\) using a justified refinement-error bound or model
    A small final correction alone does not bound the entire omitted tail
  • Pilot uncertainty: rare contributions and noisy replication variances can mislead allocation
  • Complete work: include setup, pilots, minimum sample counts, and allowed design sizes

Pilot estimates guide allocation; accuracy claims need justified error and bias assessments

For deterministic QMC, the relevant error amplitude may be difficult to know or estimate. Use a theoretical bound or a validated a posteriori procedure under its stated assumptions, such as justified coefficient-decay conditions; do not reinterpret an IID sample SD as a deterministic QMC bound. For approximation bias, a mean correction can help fit a convergence model, but a bound on the sum of omitted corrections needs assumptions on the remaining hierarchy. Quantify uncertainty in estimated correction means and model parameters. Do not choose L merely because one noisy correction is small. Independent RQMC replication supports variance estimation; very small R does not by itself support a reliable confidence interval or a finite-sample coverage guarantee. The pilot/amplitude model is for allocation and planning, not an automatic stopping certificate.

Gradient descent

Estimation

\[\begin{gather*} \vtheta_* = \argmin_{\vtheta\in\reals^m}\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta) \\ \vtheta_{k+1}=\vtheta_k-\eta_k\nabla\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_k) \end{gather*}\]

  • \(m\) parameters; \(\eta_k>0\) is the learning rate
  • Negative gradient: steepest local decrease in the Euclidean norm
  • A short enough step decreases a smooth loss when its gradient is nonzero
  • A large step can overshoot; small steps can make little progress
  • For a nonconvex loss, reaching a global minimizer is not guaranteed

A good local direction need not give a fast route to a minimum

The banana function is still difficult

\[R(\vtheta)=(1-\theta_1)^2+100(\theta_2-\theta_1^2)^2 \qquad \vtheta_*=[1,1]^\top\]

Narrow curved valley: unequal curvature slows GD even in two dimensions

Both methods start at (-1.2,1). GD uses Armijo backtracking with initial step 1, halving and sufficient-decrease constant 1e-4; its loss decreases at every accepted update. Thus the difficulty persists with a line search, not merely a poorly chosen fixed step. BFGS learns an inverse-Hessian approximation from changes in gradients. The horizontal axis counts accepted updates, not equal work: backtracking and BFGS may each evaluate functions/gradients more than once. The companion reports those counts. At the minimizer the Hessian is [[802,-400],[-400,200]], with eigenvalues about 0.399 and 1001.601, giving condition number about 2508. Noise does not round out this narrow valley.

Conditioning versus dimension

For \(\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta)=\tfrac12\vtheta^\top\mA\vtheta\) with \(\mA\) symmetric positive definite and minimizer \(\vtheta_*=\vzero\),

\[\begin{gather*} \vtheta_{k+1}=(\mI-\eta_{\mathrm{best}}\mA)\vtheta_k \qquad \kappa=\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\mA)}{\lambda_{\min}(\mA)} \\ \eta_{\mathrm{best}}=\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\min}} \qquad q=\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1} \qquad \norm{\vtheta_{k+1}}\leq q\norm{\vtheta_k} \end{gather*}\]

  • \(q\) bounds the fraction of parameter error remaining per GD update
  • \(\mA=\operatorname{diag}(1,100)\): \(\kappa=100\), \(q=99/101\approx0.9802\) — slow
  • \(\mA=\mI\) in a million dimensions: \(\kappa=1\), \(\eta_{\mathrm{best}}=1\), \(q=0\) — exact in one update
  • Large \(m\) raises computational and storage costs, independently of \(q\)
  • Scaling, preconditioning, momentum, and curvature information can help

We want small \(q\): less error remains after each update
\(q\) near \(1\): slow worst-case progress

The displayed bound is for this positive-definite quadratic in Euclidean norm, with its minimizer at zero, so norm(theta_k) is the parameter error. The contraction factor q bounds the fraction of this error remaining after one update at the displayed best constant scalar step; it is not the fraction removed and not an objective-value contraction factor. Smaller q is better: q=0 removes all parameter error in one update, while q near one allows only slow worst-case progress. In a million dimensions with A=I, every eigenvalue is one, kappa=1, eta_best=1, and q=(1-1)/(1+1)=0. The update is theta_{k+1}=(I-I)theta_k=0, regardless of the starting vector. Reading and updating a million coordinates still costs work.

Diagonalize A with an orthogonal matrix; each error component is multiplied by 1-eta*lambda. Minimizing the largest absolute factor over constant scalar steps gives q=(kappa-1)/(kappa+1). This is a sharp worst-case bound, not necessarily the fraction remaining for every starting error. It is not a convergence formula for arbitrary nonconvex objectives. For diag(1,100), eta_best=2/101 and q=99/101: about 98.02% of the parameter error may remain after an update. At eta=1/100 instead, the steep component vanishes at once but the shallow component retains 99% per update. The best worst-case scalar step balances the two extreme modes. Scaling and preconditioning change the effective curvature; momentum and curvature-aware methods change the update beyond this basic GD formula.

N and m are independent axes: a large dataset can make SGD useful with only two parameters. A huge parameter count can make full dense Newton impractical without making GD geometrically faster. A naive dense Newton linear solve costs O(m^3); sparse, structured, Hessian-vector, and limited-memory methods can avoid that cost. Reverse-mode automatic differentiation is also called adjoint-mode differentiation or a discrete adjoint method; backpropagation is the usual neural-network name. The reverse sweep applies transposed local Jacobians to propagate output sensitivities back to the inputs. Continuous adjoint equations for differential equations are related, but need not give the same numerical derivative as differentiating a particular discrete solver. See Levitt, The adjoint trick and Bindel, Automatic differentiation. Statistical prediction error and optimization error are different; optimize accurately enough for the application, and use held-out data to judge prediction. SGD is much older than deep learning; large datasets, differentiable models, automatic differentiation and parallel hardware expanded its use.

Stochastic gradient descent

Estimation

Select \(I_k\) uniformly from \(\{0,\ldots,N-1\}\), independently of previous draws

\[\begin{gather*} \vG_k=\nabla\operatorname{loss}_{I_k}(\vtheta_k) \qquad \vtheta_{k+1}=\vtheta_k-\eta_k\vG_k \\ \Ex[\vG_k\mid\mathcal F_k]=\nabla\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_k) \end{gather*}\]

Here \(\mathcal F_k\) contains the previous draws and the current iterate \(\vtheta_k\)

  • A fresh random observation gives a cheap gradient estimate
  • Conditional unbiasedness concerns the gradient at the current iterate
  • It does not make \(\vtheta_k\) an unbiased minimizer estimate
  • A particular update may increase the full loss

Estimate the gradient by Monte Carlo, then use it to update the parameters

The expectation is over the fresh sampled index, holding the history and current iterate fixed. A random permutation of the data within an epoch is also common, but its next index is not independent uniform conditional on all previous indices. Our identities and notebook experiments use sampling with replacement. They are not proofs for arbitrary shuffling or QMC substitutions.

Mini-batches and gradient noise

Estimation

With \(b\) fresh IID indices, sampled with replacement,

\[\begin{gather*} \vG_k=\frac1b\sum_{j=1}^b\nabla\operatorname{loss}_{I_{kj}}(\vtheta_k) \\ \sigma_k^2=\frac1N\sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \norm{\nabla\operatorname{loss}_i(\vtheta_k)-\nabla\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_k)}^2 \\ \Ex\bigl[\norm{\vG_k-\nabla\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_k)}^2\mid\mathcal F_k\bigr] =\frac{\sigma_k^2}{b} \end{gather*}\]

  • Larger batch: less gradient noise, more work per update
  • Even at a minimizer, individual component gradients may be nonzero
  • Vectorized batches can use hardware more efficiently than single observations

Choose \(b\) for accuracy per unit cost; fewer updates alone prove little

Expand the squared norm of the average of b centered independent vectors. Cross terms have zero conditional expectation; the b diagonal terms give sigma_k^2/b. Sigma_k^2 is the trace of the component-gradient covariance, not the loss variance. Sampling b distinct observations uniformly without replacement gives an additional (N-b)/(N-1) factor for N>1. Drawing b=N with replacement is not the full gradient; full GD explicitly visits every observation once.

Why a noisy direction can help

Assume \(\nabla\operatorname{Loss}\) is \(L\)-Lipschitz and \(0<\eta_k\leq1/L\)

\[\begin{align*} \Ex[\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_{k+1})\mid\mathcal F_k] &\leq\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_k) \\ &\quad-\underbrace{\frac{\eta_k}{2}\norm{\nabla\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_k)}^2}_{\text{downhill progress}} +\underbrace{\frac{L\eta_k^2\sigma_k^2}{2b}}_{\text{noise penalty}} \end{align*}\]

  • Far from a stationary point, downhill progress can dominate
  • Near it, a fixed learning rate can leave persistent fluctuations
  • Decrease \(\eta_k\) or increase \(b\) when finer accuracy is needed

Noisy gradients can be cheap enough to be useful; noise does not cure poor conditioning

The descent lemma gives F(theta-eta G) <= F(theta)-eta grad(F)^T G + L eta^2 ||G||^2/2. Conditional unbiasedness and the squared-norm variance decomposition give -eta(1-L eta/2)||grad(F)||^2+L eta^2 sigma_k^2/(2b). Using eta<=1/L yields the displayed simpler bound. This is an expected one-step bound under the stated assumptions, not descent on every draw or a global-minimum guarantee. Rosenbrock is not globally L-smooth on all of R^2; the bound is not asserted globally for that example. See Bottou, Curtis, and Nocedal, sections 4.1-4.3, for fuller convergence assumptions and results.

Learning rates and the noise floor

A scalar example: \(\operatorname{loss}_i(\theta)=\tfrac12(\theta-a_i)^2\) with \(a_i\in\{-1,1\}\) equally likely

\[\theta_{k+1}=(1-\eta)\theta_k+\eta\overline a_k \qquad \var(\overline a_k)=1/b\]

For constant \(0<\eta<2\) and independent batches,

\[\lim_{k\to\infty}\Ex(\theta_k^2)=\frac{\eta}{b(2-\eta)}\]

  • Smaller \(\eta\) or larger \(b\): lower limiting mean squared error
  • Decaying \(\eta_k\): progressively smaller updates, slower late progress
  • A classical schedule is \(\eta_k=\eta_0/(1+k/k_0)\)
    Convergence still needs assumptions on the objective and noise

A fixed step can be useful for early progress while limiting final precision

The mean of a is zero and its variance is one. Conditional independence gives E theta_{k+1}2=(1-eta)2 E theta_k2+eta2/b. Solve the scalar geometric recurrence to obtain the limit. Excess loss equals theta^2/2. This exact noise floor is specific to this quadratic and these nonvanishing component gradients; it is not inevitable when every component gradient vanishes at the same minimizer. For the stated decreasing schedule, positive eta_0,k_0 give sum eta_k=infinity and sum eta_k^2<infinity, classical stochastic approximation conditions. They alone do not ensure convergence without suitable smoothness, boundedness/noise and objective assumptions.

Estimation

\[\begin{gather*} p_i(\vtheta)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\vx_i^\top\vtheta}} \\ \operatorname{loss}_i(\vtheta)=\log(1+e^{\vx_i^\top\vtheta}) -y_i\vx_i^\top\vtheta+\frac{\lambda}{2}\norm{\vtheta}^2 \\ \nabla\operatorname{loss}_i(\vtheta)=(p_i(\vtheta)-y_i)\vx_i+\lambda\vtheta \end{gather*}\]

  • Applications include game moves and sequential planning

Monte Carlo estimates guide which action to take and where to search next

MCTS means Monte Carlo tree search, not Markov chain tree search. It is a sequential-decision and search method, distinct from MCMC target sampling. For this introduction use a finite-horizon, single-agent planning problem with bounded rewards and a simulator for state transitions. A return is the sum of future rewards, with discounting if specified. A fixed rollout policy estimates its own continuation value, not automatically the optimal value; continued tree expansion and improved decisions matter. Stochastic transitions can be represented by chance nodes or sampled within the simulator. Source: Kocsis and Szepesvari (2006).

Backpropagation here means updating tree statistics, not differentiating through a neural network. Each ancestor uses the return appropriate to its position on the path, including rewards and discounting when relevant. Terminal states skip expansion and rollout. A truncated rollout may use a value approximation, adding modeling error. A common final choice is the most visited root action; distinguish that decision rule from the exploratory selection rule used during tree growth. Parallel rollouts are possible, but MCTS is a search strategy with its own separate teaching purpose.
