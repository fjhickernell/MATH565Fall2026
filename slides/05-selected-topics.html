Parallel computing

Practice

Modern CPUs and GPUs can evaluate many operations concurrently

  • Monte Carlo function values are often pleasingly parallel: samples can be evaluated independently
  • Parallel random-number streams must remain nonoverlapping and statistically sound
  • GPU arithmetic is often optimized for 32-bit floating point rather than 64-bit floating point
  • Reduced precision may lose significant digits but can increase throughput substantially
  • GPUs help most when there are many similar operations
    Launch and data-transfer costs can dominate small problems

Parallelism changes computational cost

It does not repair biased estimators or poor sampling designs

Faster computation and statistical accuracy

Compare a baseline and a faster implementation: single-core / multicore CPU, or CPU / GPU
For fixed sampling method; runtime proportional to sample count

\[\begin{equation*} \left. \begin{aligned} s=\frac{\operatorname{time}_{\mathrm{baseline}}(n)}{\operatorname{time}_{\mathrm{faster}}(n)}>1 &\quad\implies\quad n_{\mathrm{faster}}\approx s\,n_{\mathrm{baseline}}\\ \operatorname{error}(n)\approx A n^{-p} &\quad\implies\quad \frac{\operatorname{error}(n_{\mathrm{faster}})}{\operatorname{error}(n_{\mathrm{baseline}})}\approx\frac1{s^p} \end{aligned} \right\} \quad\begin{gathered}\alerttext{for the same}\\\alerttext{time budget}\end{gathered} \end{equation*}\]

Error: RMSE for IID/RQMC; absolute-error bound for QMC

Method Power \(p\) Conditions
IID \(1/2\) Finite contribution variance
QMC \(\approx1\) Sufficient smoothness and a suitable design
RQMC \(\approx3/2\) Sufficient smoothness and suitable scrambling

Speedup \(s=16\): about \(1/4\) of the IID RMSE, \(1/16\) of the QMC bound, \(1/64\) of the RQMC RMSE

Float32 can eventually impose a numerical error floor; the saved experiment does not locate that floor. time_baseline(n)/time_faster(n) compares the time for the same n contributions. In the throughput regime, time is approximately proportional to sample count, so speedup s permits s times as many points at the same time budget. For a model error(n) approximately A n^{-p}, the ratio is s^{-p}. Keep the sampling method, integrand, and error constant A comparable between implementations. IID uses RMSE sigma/sqrt(n); deterministic QMC uses an absolute-error bound, not RMSE over randomizations or a ratio of actual errors. The displayed QMC/RQMC powers suppress logarithmic factors and are illustrative, not universal guarantees. QMC requires the smoothness/variation assumptions of its bound and a suitable point design. Smooth-integrand RQMC rates near n^{-3/2} require an appropriate randomization, such as nested uniform scrambling of suitable digital nets; a generic random shift does not ensure that rate. For replicated RQMC, keep the number of replications fixed. The s=16 examples use the nominal powers 1/2, 1, and 3/2. This applies to single-core versus multicore CPU implementations as well as CPU versus GPU implementations; name the actual baseline in either case. Total sample count controls sampling error; batch size controls memory and throughput. At fixed batch size, speedup can level off as both runtimes become proportional to n. Eight replications give a noisy empirical RMSE; a lucky point does not rank precision. MLMC glossary: contributions

The same notation works for \(L=2\) or many levels

Symbol Meaning
\(\ell\), \(L\) Level index and finest included level
\(d_\ell\), \(f_{d_\ell}\) Resolution and approximate output function
\(Y_\ell\) Cheap output at level 1; fine output minus coarse output at later levels
\(\mu_\ell\), \(\hmu_\ell\) Contribution mean \(\Ex(Y_\ell)\) and its estimate
\(\mu_{d_L}\) Mean of the finest approximation: \(\sum_\ell\mu_\ell\)
\(V_\ell\) IID contribution variance \(\var(Y_\ell)\)

MLMC glossary: samples and work

Symbol Meaning
\(n_\ell\), \(n=\sum_\ell n_\ell\) Level sample count and total sample count
\(\$_\ell\) Work for one complete contribution at level \(\ell\)
\(w_\ell\) Sample allocation weight: \(n_\ell/n=w_\ell/\sum_k w_k\)

Total work, for two or many levels

\[ C=n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2+\cdots+n_L\$_L =\sum_{\ell=1}^L n_\ell\$_\ell \]

\(C(\varepsilon)\) is the work needed at error tolerance \(\varepsilon\)

Sample counts and work shares differ

Introduce the method-specific amplitude A_l and decay power p only when needed in the two-level error models. For IID, A_l^2=V_l. Random methods use RMSE; deterministic QMC uses a justified absolute-error bound.

The fixed work budget C determines n; n and C are not independently imposed budgets. Work shares are proportional to cost_l w_l, whereas sample shares are proportional to w_l. Complete contribution work includes inputs, transforms, and coarse/fine evaluations with any legitimate reuse. Setup, pilots, and allowed sample-size rounding must also be charged.

For RQMC, n_l=R N_l counts all evaluations over a fixed number R of independent randomizations with N_l points in each run. Estimate variance of the complete reported mean across independent randomizations, rather than using individual output variance as an RQMC error estimate. Two-level Monte Carlo

Estimation

We want to compute \(\Ex\bigl[f_{d}(\vX_{1:d})\bigr]\) for large \(d\)

But large \(d\) costs more, e.g., \(\$\bigl(f_d(\vX_{1:d})\bigr)\propto d\)

  • Computational finance: \(d=\) time steps \(\times\) number of assets
  • ODE/PDE models with random fields: \(d=\) grid points representing the field

Choose \(d_1\ll d_2=d\); couple the outputs to reduce the variance of their difference

\[\begin{align*} Y_1&=f_{d_1}(\vX_{1:d_1})& &\alerttext{cheap}\text{ approximation}\\ Y_2&=f_{d_2}(\vX_{1:d_2})-f_{d_1}(\vX_{1:d_1})& &\alerttext{expensive}\text{ correction} \end{align*}\]

\[ \mu=\Ex\{f_{d}(\vX_{1:d})\}=\Ex(Y_1)+\Ex(Y_2) \]

Estimate the cheap part often and the expensive correction less often, if its error is easier to control

Here mu is the full two-level mean at the fixed fine resolution d=d2, equal to mu1+mu2. The hierarchy section separately distinguishes a finite fine mean from a limiting target when introducing approximation bias. The displayed linear cost is an illustrative model for evaluation, not a universal dimension law. Cost includes input generation, transforms, and evaluation; dense factorizations or other solvers can cost more than O(d). In finance, d can count the scalar random inputs for a time-by-asset path discretization. In a random-field ODE/PDE model, d can count field values on a grid; the field-generation and solver costs need not be linear in d. A complete coupled correction costs roughly the fine evaluation plus any coarse work that cannot be reused. Its cost is typically of order d2 in this illustrative setting, whereas the cheap level costs order d1. Within Y2, coarse and fine approximations share a path or compatible inputs. The two outputs being subtracted are coupled; the goal is small variance of their difference, not small variance of either output separately. If F and G denote the fine and coarse outputs within one correction, then Var(F-G)=Var(F)+Var(G)-2 Cov(F,G). A strong positive covariance can therefore reduce correction variance, even when both output variances are substantial. Coupling is a joint construction preserving the required marginal law of each output. Shared randomness alone does not guarantee a good coupling. The expectation identity remains valid because those marginal means are unchanged. The expectation here is scalar; high dimensionality describes the random input vector used to generate the output. For time discretizations, preserve Brownian values at shared times; a Brownian bridge can refine the coarse path. Between the two level estimators, use independent samples or independent RQMC randomizations. A small mean correction does not imply a small variance or a small QMC error constant. Coupling should make the correction easier to integrate; that property, together with the cost ratio, must justify two levels.

Coupling coarse and fine Brownian paths

Choose nested monitoring grids on \([0,T]\) with \(d_2=\kappa d_1\), \(\kappa>1\)

  • Order the Brownian values \(\vX\) with the coarse-grid values first
  • Use a Brownian bridge to add values at the extra fine-grid times

For an option, \(f_{d_\ell}(\vX_{1:d_\ell})\) computes its discounted payoff
using the path values at \(d_\ell\) monitoring times

\[\begin{align*} Y_1&=f_{d_1}(\vX_{1:d_1})&&\alerttext{cheap}\text{ coarse payoff}\\ Y_2&=f_{d_2}(\vX_{1:d_2})-f_{d_1}(\vX_{1:d_1})&&\alerttext{expensive}\text{ payoff correction} \end{align*}\]

  • Estimate \(\Ex(Y_1)\) from coarse-only paths
  • Estimate \(\Ex(Y_2)\) from a separate independent set of bridge-refined paths

Same path, two payoffs in \(Y_2\); aim for small variance of their difference

Here X_1:d1 contains coarse-grid Brownian values. Append the conditionally generated Brownian-bridge values at added times to obtain X_1:d2. This is hierarchical rather than chronological ordering. Each payoff function knows the monitoring times attached to its inputs and restores chronological order when needed. Thus the coarse vector is literally a prefix of the fine vector, matching the f_d notation on the preceding slide. Nested grids may be nonuniform. Counts d1 and d2 are positive integers and kappa=d2/d1>1. Equally spaced nested grids require an integer refinement factor; general nested grids allow other ratios. No bridge-generation details are needed here. The notebook’s dyadic grids illustrate one particular choice. The fine and coarse approximations use the same Brownian values at shared times; their payoffs need not agree. Generate correction paths independently of the coarse-only sample set used for the cheap mean. For RQMC, use independent randomizations between level estimates; points within a run may be dependent. The coarse-only sample has the same marginal law as the coarse part of a refined path, preserving the telescoping expectation identity. Coupling’s benefit still depends on the correction’s method-specific error.

Random two-level estimates: IID or RQMC

Estimate \(\mu_\ell=\Ex(Y_\ell)\) with uncorrelated level sample means: \(\operatorname{cov}(\hmu_1,\hmu_2)=0\)
Independent sample sets \(\{Y_{i,1}\}_{i=0}^{n_1-1}\) and \(\{Y_{i,2}\}_{i=0}^{n_2-1}\) ensure this condition

\[\begin{align*} \hmu&=\hmu_1+\hmu_2& \hmu_\ell&=\frac1{n_\ell}\sum_{i=0}^{n_\ell-1}Y_{i,\ell}\\ \var(\hmu)&=\var(\hmu_1)+\var(\hmu_2)& C&=n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2 \end{align*}\]

For unbiased level estimates, model \(\operatorname{RMSE}(\hmu_\ell)\approx A_\ell n_\ell^{-p}\)

\[ \operatorname{MSE}(\hmu) =\var(\hmu) \approx\frac{A_1^2}{n_1^{2p}}+\frac{A_2^2}{n_2^{2p}} \]

  • IID: \(p=1/2\), \(A_\ell^2=\var(Y_\ell)\); the variance model is exact
  • RQMC: \(p\) and \(A_\ell\) depend on smoothness, design, and randomization
    Points within each sample set may be dependent

The MSE here is relative to the full two-level mean mu at the fixed fine resolution. Use a positive common exponent p for this allocation model and positive amplitudes A_l. Different levels can have different rates; the common-p formulas are a simplifying model, not a claim that all rates agree. For RQMC, Y_il are values from a randomized design with the correct marginals. Unbiasedness is assumed, and the variance is over design randomizations. Variance addition requires Cov(hmu1,hmu2)=0; independence is sufficient, not necessary. The explicitly indexed sample sets can be generated independently between levels, with independent RQMC randomizations. Within-level points may be dependent. The algebraically defined Y1 and Y2 need not be uncorrelated when evaluated on the same path; the zero-covariance condition is on their separately sampled level means. Within the correction Y2, retain the coarse/fine coupling. With a fixed number R of independent runs per level, n_l can denote total evaluations; absorb fixed replication factors into A_l and keep cost per evaluation consistent. More explicitly, n_l=R N_l and Var(mean of R runs) approximately a_l^2 N_l^{-2p}/R, so the amplitude in total-count notation is A_l=a_l R^{p-1/2}. Keep R fixed when varying n_l. Smooth integrands under suitable scrambling may have p near 3/2, ignoring logarithmic factors; a generic random shift does not guarantee that rate.

Allocating work optimally

Estimation

For independent unbiased random estimates, minimize the MSE model

\[ \frac{A_1^2}{n_1^{2p}}+\frac{A_2^2}{n_2^{2p}} \quad\text{subject to}\quad n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2=C \]

The optimal continuous allocation satisfies

\[ \frac{n_1}{n_2} =\left(\frac{A_1^2\$_2}{A_2^2\$_1}\right)^{1/(2p+1)} \]

IID (\(p=1/2\), \(A_\ell^2=V_\ell\)): sample allocation weights

\[ w_\ell=\frac{A_\ell}{\sqrt{\$_\ell}}=\sqrt{\frac{V_\ell}{\$_\ell}} \qquad\frac{n_1}{n_2}=\frac{w_1}{w_2} \qquad\frac{n_\ell}{n}=\frac{w_\ell}{w_1+w_2} \]

More points for larger error amplitude; fewer for larger cost per point

For positive amplitudes and costs, differentiate the Lagrangian: \(2p A_\ell^2 n_\ell^{-(2p+1)}=\lambda\$_\ell\). The common factor \(2p\) cancels in the ratio. This is exact optimization of the continuous power-law model; RQMC power laws are generally approximations. At p=1/2 the optimal IID MSE is \((\sqrt{V_1\$_1}+\sqrt{V_2\$_2})^2/C\), as in the original IID treatment. Round to positive admissible sample sizes; RQMC may require powers of a base. Include setup and pilot costs in practical work. Pilot estimates of IID variance do not automatically identify RQMC amplitudes or rates; use whole randomized estimates and justify the applicable model.

Deriving the two-level allocation

For the random case, treat \(n_1,n_2>0\) as continuous; fix total work \(C\)

\[ \underbrace{\mathcal L}_{\text{Lagrangian}} =\frac{A_1^2}{n_1^{2p}}+\frac{A_2^2}{n_2^{2p}} +\lambda(n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2-C) \]

\[\begin{align*} \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial n_\ell}=0 &\implies\frac{2pA_\ell^2}{n_\ell^{2p+1}}=\lambda\$_\ell \qquad(\ell=1,2)\\ \frac{A_1^2}{\$_1n_1^{2p+1}} &=\frac{A_2^2}{\$_2n_2^{2p+1}} \implies\frac{n_1}{n_2} =\left(\frac{A_1^2\$_2}{A_2^2\$_1}\right)^{1/(2p+1)} \end{align*}\]

The sample allocation weights give

\[ w_\ell=\left(\frac{A_\ell^2}{\$_\ell}\right)^{1/(2p+1)} \qquad n_\ell=\frac{Cw_\ell}{\$_1w_1+\$_2w_2} =\frac{nw_\ell}{w_1+w_2} \]

At the optimum, each level buys the same marginal MSE reduction per unit work

This derives the random allocation on the preceding slide. Keep the IID marginal-MSE continuations with this derivation before the deterministic bounds. Differentiate the MSE model, not RMSE, since their minimizers agree. The magnitude of the derivative with respect to work on level ell is 2p A_ell^2/(cost_ell n_ell^(2p+1)). If these marginal gains differ, transfer a little work toward the level with the larger gain. For p>0 and positive amplitudes and costs, the objective is strictly convex and diverges at either boundary of the feasible budget segment; the stationary point is the unique global continuous minimum. RQMC still uses an approximate variance model. Round up to allowed counts when targeting a tolerance; the fixed-budget continuous solution may need adjustment for admissible sizes.

IID: MSE decrease per unit work

Treat \(n_\ell>0\) as continuous; keep \(A_\ell,\$_\ell\) fixed

\[\begin{align*} \operatorname{MSE}_\ell&=\frac{A_\ell^2}{n_\ell} &\implies\quad \frac{\mathrm d\operatorname{MSE}_\ell}{\mathrm dn_\ell} &=-\frac{A_\ell^2}{n_\ell^2}\\ \operatorname{cost}_\ell&=n_\ell\$_\ell &\implies\quad \frac{\mathrm d\operatorname{cost}_\ell}{\mathrm dn_\ell}&=\$_\ell \end{align*}\]

The MSE decrease per unit work is the ratio of these derivatives

\[ -\frac{\mathrm d\operatorname{MSE}_\ell}{\mathrm d\operatorname{cost}_\ell} =-\frac{\mathrm d\operatorname{MSE}_\ell/\mathrm dn_\ell} {\mathrm d\operatorname{cost}_\ell/\mathrm dn_\ell} =\frac{A_\ell^2}{\$_\ell n_\ell^2} \]

The derivative is evaluated in the continuous allocation model, with A_ell and cost per contribution fixed and positive. The minus sign makes the MSE decrease positive. The chain rule converts a change per extra sample into a change per unit of work. If the two derivative magnitudes differ, transfer a small amount of work toward the level with the larger magnitude; the total MSE decreases to first order. The Lagrange equations on the preceding slide set these magnitudes equal to lambda. For IID, w_ell is A_ell/sqrt(cost per contribution), so equal magnitudes imply equal w_ell/n_ell. Setup, pilots, and sample-size rounding remain outside this linear model. The next continuation connects the derivative to level RMSE and sample weights.

IID: interpreting the optimal allocation

Write \(\operatorname{err}_\ell^2=\operatorname{MSE}_\ell\) and \(w_\ell=A_\ell/\sqrt{\$_\ell}\)

\[ -\frac{\mathrm d\operatorname{MSE}_\ell}{\mathrm d\operatorname{cost}_\ell} =\frac{A_\ell^2}{\$_\ell n_\ell^2} =\frac{\operatorname{err}_\ell^2}{\operatorname{cost}_\ell} =\left(\frac{w_\ell}{n_\ell}\right)^2 \]

If one level has a larger value, moving a little work to it lowers total MSE

At the optimum, both derivative magnitudes equal \(\lambda\)

\[ \left(\frac{w_1}{n_1}\right)^2 =\left(\frac{w_2}{n_2}\right)^2=\lambda \implies\frac{n_1}{n_2}=\frac{w_1}{w_2} \]

Optimal allocation equalizes the MSE decrease per unit work

The term err_ell is the RMSE of the level sample mean, not an observed error. This interpretation uses the continuous IID model on the preceding slide. Positive weights and sample counts let us take square roots of the equality.

Deterministic two-level estimates

Use QMC separately for the same cheap part and coupled correction

Assume justified absolute-error bounds with a common \(p>0\)

\[\begin{align*} \abs{\hmu_\ell-\mu_\ell}&\le\frac{A_\ell}{n_\ell^p}\\ \abs{\hmu-\mu} &\le\abs{\hmu_1-\mu_1}+\abs{\hmu_2-\mu_2} \le\frac{A_1}{n_1^p}+\frac{A_2}{n_2^p} \end{align*}\]

At fixed work \(n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2=C\), minimize the bound

\[ w_\ell=\left(\frac{A_\ell}{\$_\ell}\right)^{1/(p+1)} \qquad\frac{n_1}{n_2}=\frac{w_1}{w_2} =\left(\frac{A_1\$_2}{A_2\$_1}\right)^{1/(p+1)} \]

QMC needs sufficient smoothness and a suitable design
Bound total error by the sum of the level error bounds

This is a triangle-inequality upper bound, not a sum-of-variances identity or an equality for the realized integration error. No independence or unbiasedness is asserted for deterministic estimates. Minimize the stated upper bound, not the unknown actual error. Differentiation gives \(p A_\ell n_\ell^{-(p+1)}=\lambda\$_\ell\) and the displayed ratio. A_l here is a deterministic error-bound amplitude, not the RQMC amplitude or the contribution SD. For a suitable RKHS bound it may be a discrepancy rate constant times the variation of the complete level integrand. An illustrative rate is p near 1 with appropriate smoothness and design; retain logarithmic factors if needed. A small correction variance alone does not guarantee a small deterministic QMC error amplitude. This bound is relative to the full two-level mean mu at the fixed fine resolution. Limiting-target approximation bias is treated separately in the hierarchy section.

What two levels accomplish

  • \(d_1\ll d_2\): the coarse approximation can be much cheaper
  • Coupling can make the expensive correction easier to integrate
  • Allocate points using the error amplitudes, rates, and complete evaluation costs
  • IID/RQMC: independent unbiased level estimates add variances
  • Deterministic QMC: level error bounds add by an inequality
  • Fix the target dimension \(d_2\); increase \(n_1,n_2\) to control integration error

Two levels help when the cheap approximation and small correction error earn their combined cost

The correction must be small in the sense relevant to the integration method: variance for IID, randomized-estimate error amplitude for RQMC, and a justified variation/discrepancy bound for deterministic QMC. Neither a small correction mean nor pointwise closeness alone establishes all these properties.

Two-level random cost as a function of error

Fix grids, amplitudes, and costs; use the IID or RQMC model with common \(p>0\)

Scale the optimal counts together: \(n_1=t w_1\), \(n_2=t w_2\), \(t>0\)

From the optimal-weight definition: \(A_1^2=\$_1w_1^{2p+1}\) and \(A_2^2=\$_2w_2^{2p+1}\)

\[\begin{align*} S&=\$_1w_1+\$_2w_2 \qquad C=n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2=tS\\ \operatorname{MSE}(\hmu) &\approx\frac{A_1^2}{(tw_1)^{2p}}+\frac{A_2^2}{(tw_2)^{2p}}\\ &=\frac{\$_1w_1^{2p+1}}{t^{2p}w_1^{2p}} +\frac{\$_2w_2^{2p+1}}{t^{2p}w_2^{2p}} =\frac{\$_1w_1+\$_2w_2}{t^{2p}}=\frac{S}{t^{2p}} \end{align*}\]

\[ \operatorname{RMSE}(\hmu)\approx\frac{\sqrt S}{t^p}\approx\varepsilon \implies t\approx\left(\frac{\sqrt S}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/p} \]

\[ C(\varepsilon)=tS \approx S\left(\frac{\sqrt S}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/p} =S^{1+1/(2p)}\varepsilon^{-1/p} \]

Use unbiased, uncorrelated level estimates and the same positive sampling-error power p on both levels. The optimal weights fix the ratio of the two sample counts; the single scale t controls their size and the total work. The weight definitions give A12=cost1*w1(2p+1) and A22=cost2*w2(2p+1), making the cancellation explicit for both levels. The visible link returns to the weight definition: raise each weight to the power 2p+1, then multiply by its per-contribution cost. Set the modeled RMSE equal to the requested tolerance to find the required scale, then multiply by S to obtain total work. The general random model is approximate. For IID, p=1/2, A1^2=V1, and A2^2=V2, so it gives exactly C(epsilon)=(sqrt(V1cost1)+sqrt(V2cost2))2/epsilon2 in the continuous model; the next slide repeats this IID formula for the direct-fine comparison. All formulas omit setup, pilots, and integer or design-size rounding. This calculation concerns error relative to the fixed fine mean mu only.

When does two-level IID save work?

Compare estimates of the same fine target \(\mu\) at RMSE \(\varepsilon\)

Direct fine contributions have variance \(V\) and complete per-sample work \(\$\)

\[ C_{\mathrm{single}}(\varepsilon)=\frac{V\$}{\varepsilon^2} \qquad C_{\mathrm{two}}(\varepsilon) =\frac{\left(\sqrt{V_1\$_1}+\sqrt{V_2\$_2}\right)^2}{\varepsilon^2} \]

With optimal two-level allocation, we save work precisely when

\[ \left(\sqrt{V_1\$_1}+\sqrt{V_2\$_2}\right)^2<V\$ \]

The same \(\varepsilon^{-2}\) factor cancels; compare the cost constants

V is the variance of one direct evaluation of f_d2(X_1:d2), and dollar is the work for generating its inputs, transforming them, and evaluating that fine payoff. It need not equal dollar_2: a correction also evaluates the coarse payoff, allowing reuse. Both strategies target the same finite mean, so no approximation bias enters this RMSE comparison. The formulas are not based on V=V1+V2: the shared-path coarse payoff and correction may be correlated. Independence is imposed between the separately sampled level means. The formulas are exact in the continuous IID allocation model with independent level means; they omit setup, pilots, integer rounding, and minimum sample counts. Such additional costs must be included in a practical savings comparison.

Why the two-level cost constant can be smaller

On one shared path, the fine payoff is \(Y_1+Y_2\), so

\[ V=\var(Y_1+Y_2)=V_1+V_2+2\sqrt{V_1V_2}\corr(Y_1,Y_2) \]

  • If coupling makes \(V_2\ll V_1\), then \(V\approx V_1\)
  • If the extra coarse work is small, then \(\$_2\approx\$\)

Under these approximations,

\[ \frac{C_{\mathrm{two}}(\varepsilon)}{C_{\mathrm{single}}(\varepsilon)} \approx \left(\sqrt{\frac{\$_1}{\$_2}}+\sqrt{\frac{V_2}{V_1}}\right)^2 \]

Cheap coarse work and small correction variance can make this ratio less than one

The correlation concerns payoffs on one shared path; level sample means are sampled independently. The correlation is bounded between -1 and 1, so V1 is the leading term when V2 is much smaller than V1. \[\max_{\vtheta}\prod_i q_i(\vtheta) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \min_{\vtheta}\sum_i[-\log q_i(\vtheta)]\]

The logistic function supplies probabilities, not a unique loss function. Here the conditional label model is Bernoulli, given the covariates and theta, and labels are assumed conditionally independent. Maximum likelihood then motivates the negative log likelihood: log is increasing, and changing sign turns maximization into minimization. Each q_i is the probability of the label actually observed, not always the probability p_i of label 1. Regularization is a separate choice introduced after deriving the data loss.

Derive the logistic loss

The negative log likelihood for one observation is

\[\ell_i^{\mathrm{data}}=-\log q_i =-y_i\log p_i-(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\]

Substitute \(p_i=e^{z_i}/(1+e^{z_i})\)

\[\begin{align*} \log p_i&=z_i-\log(1+e^{z_i})\\ \log(1-p_i)&=-\log(1+e^{z_i}) \end{align*}\]

Therefore

\[\begin{align*} \ell_i^{\mathrm{data}} &=-y_i z_i+[y_i+(1-y_i)]\log(1+e^{z_i})\\ &=\log(1+e^{z_i})-y_i z_i \end{align*}\]

At \(p_i=0.9\): loss \(0.105\) for label 1; loss \(2.303\) for label 0

All logs are natural. For y=1 the loss is -log p; for y=0 it is -log(1-p). A confident wrong label has a large loss. The cancellation uses y+(1-y)=1. The final expression is evaluated using logaddexp(0,z) in the notebook to avoid overflow; it is the same objective, without clipping probabilities. This data loss is also commonly called binary cross entropy. That name is not needed to follow the Bernoulli likelihood derivation.

Regularization and the gradient

Add a separate quadratic penalty to the mean data loss

\[F(\vtheta)=\frac1N\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\ell_i^{\mathrm{data}}(\vtheta) +\frac\lambda2\norm{\vtheta}^2\]

First differentiate with respect to \(z_i=\vx_i^\top\vtheta\)

\[\frac{\dif\ell_i^{\mathrm{data}}}{\dif z_i} =\frac{e^{z_i}}{1+e^{z_i}}-y_i=p_i-y_i\]

With \(\operatorname{loss}_i=\ell_i^{\mathrm{data}}+(\lambda/2)\norm{\vtheta}^2\), the chain rule gives

\[\nabla\operatorname{loss}_i(\vtheta) =(p_i-y_i)\vx_i+\lambda\vtheta\]

  • Average these component gradients for the full gradient
  • Average a sampled batch for SGD
  • Penalizing every coefficient with \(\lambda>0\) gives a unique minimizer

Define loss_i=ell_idata+lambda||theta||2/2; its average is exactly F. The penalty appears once after averaging, not N times, and its gradient must not be divided by the batch size again. The notebook deliberately regularizes every coefficient including the intercept, so its strong convexity parameter is at least lambda. The function remains nonquadratic. The generating beta is not the empirical minimizer; L-BFGS supplies a reference only for benchmarking. This is a mean-loss convention for lambda. Deterministic games and search

Chess has deterministic moves; this toy game shows why replies matter

  • Our turns seek high values; opposing turns seek low values from our viewpoint
  • Random rollouts explore a small part of a huge, deterministic game tree

The pictured fragment is a constructed two-player, zero-sum game, not a legal chess position. Leaves are toy terminal outcomes: +1 for our win, 0 for a draw and -1 for our loss. Against an opponent who chooses the worst outcome for us, A has value min(+1,-1)=-1, while B has min(0,0)=0; we choose max(-1,0)=0, hence B. Maximizing our value at both levels would mistakenly count on the opponent allowing the win after A. This explains the objective; it is not a claim that finite-budget MCTS computes exact minimax values.

Chess transitions are deterministic given the full state (including turn, castling/en-passant rights and relevant repetition/move-count history) and the chosen legal move. The opponent’s reply is an adversarial decision, not a chance outcome with an intrinsic probability. We may model or sample replies using a policy during search, but an average against sampled replies is not automatically the value against perfect opposition. A complete terminal move sequence has a fixed outcome, even when our algorithm sampled that sequence. Uncertainty comes from the unsearched alternatives and our limited evaluation, rather than random physics in the board update.

MCTS for two-player games must account for whose turn it is, for example by changing the value sign in a player-to-move representation. The UCT paper’s game experiments use negative estimated values at MIN nodes. Neural-network-guided variants can evaluate a leaf instead of completing a rollout; the AlphaGo/AlphaZero distinction is on the next slide. A full game tree grows through both players’ legal replies, motivating selective search. Our route notebook is single-agent and does not implement adversarial turns.
