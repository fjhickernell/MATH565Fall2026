Parallel computing

Practice

Modern CPUs and GPUs can evaluate many operations concurrently

  • Monte Carlo function values are often pleasingly parallel: samples can be evaluated independently
  • Parallel random-number streams must remain nonoverlapping and statistically sound
  • GPU arithmetic is often optimized for 32-bit floating point rather than 64-bit floating point
  • Reduced precision may lose significant digits but can increase throughput substantially
  • GPUs help most when there are many similar operations
    Launch and data-transfer costs can dominate small problems

Parallelism changes computational cost

It does not repair biased estimators or poor sampling designs

Reading the CPU/GPU experiment

  • Read speedup as CPU time / GPU time: above one favors the GPU
  • Name the baseline: NumPy CPU64, NumPy CPU32, or Torch CPU32
    The ratio compares complete implementations, including their random generators
  • Small jobs can favor CPUs; larger jobs spread fixed GPU overhead over more work
  • Generation can dominate: inspect stage timings before attributing a gap to evaluation

Faster computation and statistical accuracy

Compare a baseline and a faster implementation: single-core / multicore CPU, or CPU / GPU
For fixed sampling method; runtime proportional to sample count

\[\begin{equation*} \left. \begin{aligned} s=\frac{\operatorname{time}_{\mathrm{baseline}}(n)}{\operatorname{time}_{\mathrm{faster}}(n)}>1 &\quad\implies\quad n_{\mathrm{faster}}\approx s\,n_{\mathrm{baseline}}\\ \operatorname{error}(n)\approx A n^{-p} &\quad\implies\quad \frac{\operatorname{error}(n_{\mathrm{faster}})}{\operatorname{error}(n_{\mathrm{baseline}})}\approx\frac1{s^p} \end{aligned} \right\} \quad\begin{gathered}\alerttext{for the same}\\\alerttext{time budget}\end{gathered} \end{equation*}\]

Error: RMSE for IID/RQMC; absolute-error bound for QMC

Method Power \(p\) Conditions
IID \(1/2\) Finite contribution variance
QMC \(\approx1\) Sufficient smoothness and a suitable design
RQMC \(\approx3/2\) Sufficient smoothness and suitable scrambling

Speedup \(s=16\): about \(1/4\) of the IID RMSE, \(1/16\) of the QMC bound, \(1/64\) of the RQMC RMSE

Assume negligible floating-point error and comparable variance, and ignore setup/overhead in the same-time calculation. Float32 can eventually impose a numerical error floor; the saved experiment does not locate that floor. time_baseline(n)/time_faster(n) compares the time for the same n contributions. In the throughput regime, time is approximately proportional to sample count, so speedup s permits s times as many points at the same time budget. For a model error(n) approximately A n^{-p}, the ratio is s^{-p}. Keep the sampling method, integrand, and error constant A comparable between implementations. IID uses RMSE sigma/sqrt(n); deterministic QMC uses an absolute-error bound, not RMSE over randomizations or a ratio of actual errors. The displayed QMC/RQMC powers suppress logarithmic factors and are illustrative, not universal guarantees. QMC requires the smoothness/variation assumptions of its bound and a suitable point design. Smooth-integrand RQMC rates near n^{-3/2} require an appropriate randomization, such as nested uniform scrambling of suitable digital nets; a generic random shift does not ensure that rate. For replicated RQMC, keep the number of replications fixed. The s=16 examples use the nominal powers 1/2, 1, and 3/2. This applies to single-core versus multicore CPU implementations as well as CPU versus GPU implementations; name the actual baseline in either case. Total sample count controls sampling error; batch size controls memory and throughput. At fixed batch size, speedup can level off as both runtimes become proportional to n. Eight replications give a noisy empirical RMSE; a lucky point does not rank precision. In the M3 fixed-n sweep, changing batches from 4096 to 262144 reduces GPU runtime from 34.22 to 3.92 ms without changing n=1048576 or its IID error law. Larger batches need not always help; choose using measurements and memory limits.

Computing many expectations together

  • Reuse one sample batch across many functions to share generation cost and expose matrix work

MLMC glossary: samples and work

Symbol Meaning
\(n_\ell\), \(n=\sum_\ell n_\ell\) Level sample count and total sample count
\(\$_\ell\) Work for one complete contribution at level \(\ell\)
\(w_\ell\) Sample allocation weight: \(n_\ell/n=w_\ell/\sum_k w_k\)

Total work, for two or many levels

\[ C=n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2+\cdots+n_L\$_L =\sum_{\ell=1}^L n_\ell\$_\ell \]

\(C(\varepsilon)\) is the work needed at error tolerance \(\varepsilon\)

Sample counts and work shares differ

Introduce the method-specific amplitude A_l and decay power p only when needed in the two-level error models. For IID, A_l^2=V_l. Random methods use RMSE; deterministic QMC uses a justified absolute-error bound.

The fixed work budget C determines n; n and C are not independently imposed budgets. Work shares are proportional to cost_l w_l, whereas sample shares are proportional to w_l. Complete contribution work includes inputs, transforms, and coarse/fine evaluations with any legitimate reuse. Setup, pilots, and allowed sample-size rounding must also be charged.

For RQMC, n_l=R N_l counts all evaluations over a fixed number R of independent randomizations with N_l points in each run. Two-level Monte Carlo

Estimation

We want to compute \(\Ex\bigl[f_{d}(\vX_{1:d})\bigr]\) for large \(d\)

But large \(d\) costs more, e.g., \(\$\bigl(f_d(\vX_{1:d})\bigr)\propto d\)

  • Computational finance: \(d=\) time steps \(\times\) number of assets
  • ODE/PDE models with random fields: \(d=\) grid points representing the field

Choose \(d_1\ll d_2 = d\) and couple the coarse and fine approximations

\[\begin{align*} Y_1&=f_{d_1}(\vX_{1:d_1})& &\alerttext{cheap}\text{ approximation}\\ Y_2&=f_{d_2}(\vX_{1:d_2})-f_{d_1}(\vX_{1:d_1})& &\alerttext{expensive}\text{ correction} \end{align*}\]

\[ \mu=\Ex\{f_{d}(\vX_{1:d})\}=\Ex(Y_1)+\Ex(Y_2) \]

Estimate the cheap part often and the expensive correction less often, if its error is easier to control

Here mu is the full two-level mean at the fixed fine resolution d=d2, equal to mu1+mu2. The hierarchy section separately distinguishes a finite fine mean from a limiting target when introducing approximation bias. The displayed linear cost is an illustrative model for evaluation, not a universal dimension law. Cost includes input generation, transforms, and evaluation; dense factorizations or other solvers can cost more than O(d). In finance, d can count the scalar random inputs for a time-by-asset path discretization. In a random-field ODE/PDE model, d can count field values on a grid; the field-generation and solver costs need not be linear in d. A complete coupled correction costs roughly the fine evaluation plus any coarse work that cannot be reused. Its cost is typically of order d2 in this illustrative setting, whereas the cheap level costs order d1. Within Y2, coarse and fine approximations share a path or compatible inputs. For time discretizations, preserve Brownian values at shared times; a Brownian bridge can refine the coarse path. Between the two level estimators, use independent samples or independent RQMC randomizations. A small mean correction does not imply a small variance or a small QMC error constant. Coupling should make the correction easier to integrate; that property, together with the cost ratio, must justify two levels.

Coupling coarse and fine Brownian paths

Choose nested monitoring grids on \([0,T]\) with \(d_2=\kappa d_1\), \(\kappa>1\)

  • Order the Brownian values \(\vX\) with the coarse-grid values first
  • Use a Brownian bridge to add values at the extra fine-grid times

For an option, \(f_{d_\ell}(\vX_{1:d_\ell})\) computes its discounted payoff
using the path values at \(d_\ell\) monitoring times

\[\begin{align*} Y_1&=f_{d_1}(\vX_{1:d_1})&&\alerttext{cheap}\text{ coarse payoff}\\ Y_2&=f_{d_2}(\vX_{1:d_2})-f_{d_1}(\vX_{1:d_1})&&\alerttext{expensive}\text{ payoff correction} \end{align*}\]

  • Estimate \(\Ex(Y_1)\) from coarse-only paths
  • Estimate \(\Ex(Y_2)\) from a separate independent set of bridge-refined paths

The two payoffs in \(Y_2\) use the same Brownian path at two resolutions

Here X_1:d1 contains coarse-grid Brownian values. Append the conditionally generated Brownian-bridge values at added times to obtain X_1:d2. This is hierarchical rather than chronological ordering. Each payoff function knows the monitoring times attached to its inputs and restores chronological order when needed. Thus the coarse vector is literally a prefix of the fine vector, matching the f_d notation on the preceding slide. Nested grids may be nonuniform. Counts d1 and d2 are positive integers and kappa=d2/d1>1. Equally spaced nested grids require an integer refinement factor; general nested grids allow other ratios. No bridge-generation details are needed here. The notebook’s dyadic grids illustrate one particular choice. The fine and coarse approximations use the same Brownian values at shared times; their payoffs need not agree. Generate correction paths independently of the coarse-only sample set used for the cheap mean. For RQMC, use independent randomizations between level estimates; points within a run may be dependent. The coarse-only sample has the same marginal law as the coarse part of a refined path, preserving the telescoping expectation identity. Coupling’s benefit still depends on the correction’s method-specific error.

Random two-level estimates: IID or RQMC

Estimate \(\mu_\ell=\Ex(Y_\ell)\) with uncorrelated level sample means: \(\operatorname{cov}(\hmu_1,\hmu_2)=0\)
Independent sample sets \(\{Y_{i,1}\}_{i=0}^{n_1-1}\) and \(\{Y_{i,2}\}_{i=0}^{n_2-1}\) ensure this condition

\[\begin{align*} \hmu&=\hmu_1+\hmu_2& \hmu_\ell&=\frac1{n_\ell}\sum_{i=0}^{n_\ell-1}Y_{i,\ell}\\ \var(\hmu)&=\var(\hmu_1)+\var(\hmu_2)& C&=n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2 \end{align*}\]

For unbiased level estimates, model \(\operatorname{RMSE}(\hmu_\ell)\approx A_\ell n_\ell^{-p}\)

\[ \operatorname{MSE}(\hmu) =\var(\hmu) \approx\frac{A_1^2}{n_1^{2p}}+\frac{A_2^2}{n_2^{2p}} \]

  • IID: \(p=1/2\), \(A_\ell^2=\var(Y_\ell)\); the variance model is exact
  • RQMC: \(p\) and \(A_\ell\) depend on smoothness, design, and randomization
    Points within each sample set may be dependent

The MSE here is relative to the full two-level mean mu at the fixed fine resolution. Use a positive common exponent p for this allocation model and positive amplitudes A_l. Different levels can have different rates; the common-p formulas are a simplifying model, not a claim that all rates agree. For RQMC, Y_il are values from a randomized design with the correct marginals. Unbiasedness is assumed, and the variance is over design randomizations. Variance addition requires Cov(hmu1,hmu2)=0; independence is sufficient, not necessary. The explicitly indexed sample sets can be generated independently between levels, with independent RQMC randomizations. Within-level points may be dependent. The algebraically defined Y1 and Y2 need not be uncorrelated when evaluated on the same path; the zero-covariance condition is on their separately sampled level means. Within the correction Y2, retain the coarse/fine coupling. With a fixed number R of independent runs per level, n_l can denote total evaluations; absorb fixed replication factors into A_l and keep cost per evaluation consistent. More explicitly, n_l=R N_l and Var(mean of R runs) approximately a_l^2 N_l^{-2p}/R, so the amplitude in total-count notation is A_l=a_l R^{p-1/2}. Keep R fixed when varying n_l. Smooth integrands under suitable scrambling may have p near 3/2, ignoring logarithmic factors; a generic random shift does not guarantee that rate.

Allocating work optimally

Estimation

For independent unbiased random estimates, minimize the MSE model

\[ \frac{A_1^2}{n_1^{2p}}+\frac{A_2^2}{n_2^{2p}} \quad\text{subject to}\quad n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2=C \]

The optimal continuous allocation satisfies

\[ \frac{n_1}{n_2} =\left(\frac{A_1^2\$_2}{A_2^2\$_1}\right)^{1/(2p+1)} \]

IID (\(p=1/2\), \(A_\ell^2=V_\ell\)): sample allocation weights

\[ w_\ell=\frac{A_\ell}{\sqrt{\$_\ell}}=\sqrt{\frac{V_\ell}{\$_\ell}} \qquad\frac{n_1}{n_2}=\frac{w_1}{w_2} \qquad\frac{n_\ell}{n}=\frac{w_\ell}{w_1+w_2} \]

More points for larger error amplitude; fewer for larger cost per point

For positive amplitudes and costs, differentiate the Lagrangian: \(2p A_\ell^2 n_\ell^{-(2p+1)}=\lambda\$_\ell\). The common factor \(2p\) cancels in the ratio. This is exact optimization of the continuous power-law model; RQMC power laws are generally approximations. At p=1/2 the optimal IID MSE is \((\sqrt{V_1\$_1}+\sqrt{V_2\$_2})^2/C\), as in the original IID treatment. Round to positive admissible sample sizes; RQMC may require powers of a base. Include setup and pilot costs in practical work. Pilot estimates of IID variance do not automatically identify RQMC amplitudes or rates; use whole randomized estimates and justify the applicable model.

Deriving the two-level allocation

For the random case, treat \(n_1,n_2>0\) as continuous; fix total work \(C\)

\[ \underbrace{\mathcal L}_{\text{Lagrangian}} =\frac{A_1^2}{n_1^{2p}}+\frac{A_2^2}{n_2^{2p}} +\lambda(n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2-C) \]

\[\begin{align*} \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial n_\ell}=0 &\implies\frac{2pA_\ell^2}{n_\ell^{2p+1}}=\lambda\$_\ell \qquad(\ell=1,2)\\ \frac{A_1^2}{\$_1n_1^{2p+1}} &=\frac{A_2^2}{\$_2n_2^{2p+1}} \implies\frac{n_1}{n_2} =\left(\frac{A_1^2\$_2}{A_2^2\$_1}\right)^{1/(2p+1)} \end{align*}\]

The sample allocation weights give

\[ w_\ell=\left(\frac{A_\ell^2}{\$_\ell}\right)^{1/(2p+1)} \qquad n_\ell=\frac{Cw_\ell}{\$_1w_1+\$_2w_2} =\frac{nw_\ell}{w_1+w_2} \]

At the optimum, each level buys the same marginal MSE reduction per unit work

This derives the random allocation on the preceding slide. Keep the IID marginal-MSE continuations with this derivation before the deterministic bounds. Differentiate the MSE model, not RMSE, since their minimizers agree. The magnitude of the derivative with respect to work on level ell is 2p A_ell^2/(cost_ell n_ell^(2p+1)). If these marginal gains differ, transfer a little work toward the level with the larger gain. For p>0 and positive amplitudes and costs, the objective is strictly convex and diverges at either boundary of the feasible budget segment; the stationary point is the unique global continuous minimum. RQMC still uses an approximate variance model. Round up to allowed counts when targeting a tolerance; the fixed-budget continuous solution may need adjustment for admissible sizes.

IID: MSE decrease per unit work

Treat \(n_\ell>0\) as continuous; keep \(A_\ell,\$_\ell\) fixed

\[\begin{align*} \operatorname{MSE}_\ell&=\frac{A_\ell^2}{n_\ell} &\implies\quad \frac{\mathrm d\operatorname{MSE}_\ell}{\mathrm dn_\ell} &=-\frac{A_\ell^2}{n_\ell^2}\\ \operatorname{cost}_\ell&=n_\ell\$_\ell &\implies\quad \frac{\mathrm d\operatorname{cost}_\ell}{\mathrm dn_\ell}&=\$_\ell \end{align*}\]

The MSE decrease per unit work is the ratio of these derivatives

\[ -\frac{\mathrm d\operatorname{MSE}_\ell}{\mathrm d\operatorname{cost}_\ell} =-\frac{\mathrm d\operatorname{MSE}_\ell/\mathrm dn_\ell} {\mathrm d\operatorname{cost}_\ell/\mathrm dn_\ell} =\frac{A_\ell^2}{\$_\ell n_\ell^2} \]

The derivative is evaluated in the continuous allocation model, with A_ell and cost per contribution fixed and positive. The minus sign makes the MSE decrease positive. The chain rule converts a change per extra sample into a change per unit of work. If the two derivative magnitudes differ, transfer a small amount of work toward the level with the larger magnitude; the total MSE decreases to first order. The Lagrange equations on the preceding slide set these magnitudes equal to lambda. For IID, w_ell is A_ell/sqrt(cost per contribution), so equal magnitudes imply equal w_ell/n_ell. Setup, pilots, and sample-size rounding remain outside this linear model. The next continuation connects the derivative to level RMSE and sample weights.

IID: interpreting the optimal allocation

Write \(\operatorname{err}_\ell^2=\operatorname{MSE}_\ell\) and \(w_\ell=A_\ell/\sqrt{\$_\ell}\)

\[ -\frac{\mathrm d\operatorname{MSE}_\ell}{\mathrm d\operatorname{cost}_\ell} =\frac{A_\ell^2}{\$_\ell n_\ell^2} =\frac{\operatorname{err}_\ell^2}{\operatorname{cost}_\ell} =\left(\frac{w_\ell}{n_\ell}\right)^2 \]

If one level has a larger value, moving a little work to it lowers total MSE

At the optimum, both derivative magnitudes equal \(\lambda\)

\[ \left(\frac{w_1}{n_1}\right)^2 =\left(\frac{w_2}{n_2}\right)^2=\lambda \implies\frac{n_1}{n_2}=\frac{w_1}{w_2} \]

Optimal allocation equalizes the MSE decrease per unit work

The term err_ell is the RMSE of the level sample mean, not an observed error. This interpretation uses the continuous IID model on the preceding slide. Positive weights and sample counts let us take square roots of the equality.

Deterministic two-level estimates

Use QMC separately for the same cheap part and coupled correction

Assume justified absolute-error bounds with a common \(p>0\)

\[\begin{align*} \abs{\hmu_\ell-\mu_\ell}&\le\frac{A_\ell}{n_\ell^p}\\ \abs{\hmu-\mu} &\le\abs{\hmu_1-\mu_1}+\abs{\hmu_2-\mu_2} \le\frac{A_1}{n_1^p}+\frac{A_2}{n_2^p} \end{align*}\]

At fixed work \(n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2=C\), minimize the bound

\[ w_\ell=\left(\frac{A_\ell}{\$_\ell}\right)^{1/(p+1)} \qquad\frac{n_1}{n_2}=\frac{w_1}{w_2} =\left(\frac{A_1\$_2}{A_2\$_1}\right)^{1/(p+1)} \]

QMC needs sufficient smoothness and a suitable design
Bound total error by the sum of the level error bounds

This is a triangle-inequality upper bound, not a sum-of-variances identity or an equality for the realized integration error. No independence or unbiasedness is asserted for deterministic estimates. Minimize the stated upper bound, not the unknown actual error. Differentiation gives \(p A_\ell n_\ell^{-(p+1)}=\lambda\$_\ell\) and the displayed ratio. A_l here is a deterministic error-bound amplitude, not the RQMC amplitude or the contribution SD. For a suitable RKHS bound it may be a discrepancy rate constant times the variation of the complete level integrand. An illustrative rate is p near 1 with appropriate smoothness and design; retain logarithmic factors if needed. A small correction variance alone does not guarantee a small deterministic QMC error amplitude. This bound is relative to the full two-level mean mu at the fixed fine resolution. Limiting-target approximation bias is treated separately in the hierarchy section.

What two levels accomplish

  • \(d_1\ll d_2\): the coarse approximation can be much cheaper
  • Coupling can make the expensive correction easier to integrate
  • Allocate points using the error amplitudes, rates, and complete evaluation costs
  • IID/RQMC: independent unbiased level estimates add variances
  • Deterministic QMC: level error bounds add by an inequality
  • Fix the target dimension \(d_2\); increase \(n_1,n_2\) to control integration error

Two levels help when the cheap approximation and small correction error earn their combined cost

The correction must be small in the sense relevant to the integration method: variance for IID, randomized-estimate error amplitude for RQMC, and a justified variation/discrepancy bound for deterministic QMC. Neither a small correction mean nor pointwise closeness alone establishes all these properties.

Two-level random cost as a function of error

Fix grids, amplitudes, and costs; use the IID or RQMC model with common \(p>0\)

Scale the optimal counts together: \(n_1=t w_1\), \(n_2=t w_2\), \(t>0\)

From the optimal-weight definition: \(A_1^2=\$_1w_1^{2p+1}\) and \(A_2^2=\$_2w_2^{2p+1}\)

\[\begin{align*} S&=\$_1w_1+\$_2w_2 \qquad C=n_1\$_1+n_2\$_2=tS\\ \operatorname{MSE}(\hmu) &\approx\frac{A_1^2}{(tw_1)^{2p}}+\frac{A_2^2}{(tw_2)^{2p}}\\ &=\frac{\$_1w_1^{2p+1}}{t^{2p}w_1^{2p}} +\frac{\$_2w_2^{2p+1}}{t^{2p}w_2^{2p}} =\frac{\$_1w_1+\$_2w_2}{t^{2p}}=\frac{S}{t^{2p}} \end{align*}\]

\[ \operatorname{RMSE}(\hmu)\approx\frac{\sqrt S}{t^p}\approx\varepsilon \implies t\approx\left(\frac{\sqrt S}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/p} \]

\[ C(\varepsilon)=tS \approx S\left(\frac{\sqrt S}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/p} =S^{1+1/(2p)}\varepsilon^{-1/p} \]

Use unbiased, uncorrelated level estimates and the same positive sampling-error power p on both levels. The optimal weights fix the ratio of the two sample counts; the single scale t controls their size and the total work. The weight definitions give A12=cost1*w1(2p+1) and A22=cost2*w2(2p+1), making the cancellation explicit for both levels. The visible link returns to the weight definition: raise each weight to the power 2p+1, then multiply by its per-contribution cost. Set the modeled RMSE equal to the requested tolerance to find the required scale, then multiply by S to obtain total work. The general random model is approximate. For IID, p=1/2, A1^2=V1, and A2^2=V2, so it gives exactly C(epsilon)=(sqrt(V1cost1)+sqrt(V2cost2))2/epsilon2 in the continuous model; the next slide repeats this IID formula for the direct-fine comparison. All formulas omit setup, pilots, and integer or design-size rounding. This calculation concerns error relative to the fixed fine mean mu only.

When does two-level IID save work?

Compare estimates of the same fine target \(\mu\) at RMSE \(\varepsilon\)

Direct fine contributions have variance \(V\) and complete per-sample work \(\$\)

\[ C_{\mathrm{single}}(\varepsilon)=\frac{V\$}{\varepsilon^2} \qquad C_{\mathrm{two}}(\varepsilon) =\frac{\left(\sqrt{V_1\$_1}+\sqrt{V_2\$_2}\right)^2}{\varepsilon^2} \]

With optimal two-level allocation, we save work precisely when

\[ \left(\sqrt{V_1\$_1}+\sqrt{V_2\$_2}\right)^2<V\$ \]

The same \(\varepsilon^{-2}\) factor cancels; compare the cost constants

V is the variance of one direct evaluation of f_d2(X_1:d2), and dollar is the work for generating its inputs, transforming them, and evaluating that fine payoff. It need not equal dollar_2: a correction also evaluates the coarse payoff, allowing reuse. Both strategies target the same finite mean, so no approximation bias enters this RMSE comparison. The formulas are not based on V=V1+V2: the shared-path coarse payoff and correction may be correlated. Independence is imposed between the separately sampled level means. The formulas are exact in the continuous IID allocation model with independent level means; they omit setup, pilots, integer rounding, and minimum sample counts. Such additional costs must be included in a practical savings comparison.

Why the two-level cost constant can be smaller

On one shared path, the fine payoff is \(Y_1+Y_2\), so

\[ V=\var(Y_1+Y_2)=V_1+V_2+2\sqrt{V_1V_2}\corr(Y_1,Y_2) \]

  • If coupling makes \(V_2\ll V_1\), then \(V\approx V_1\)
  • If the extra coarse work is small, then \(\$_2\approx\$\)

Under these approximations,

\[ \frac{C_{\mathrm{two}}(\varepsilon)}{C_{\mathrm{single}}(\varepsilon)} \approx \left(\sqrt{\frac{\$_1}{\$_2}}+\sqrt{\frac{V_2}{V_1}}\right)^2 \]

Cheap coarse work and small correction variance can make this ratio less than one

The correlation concerns payoffs on one shared path; level sample means are sampled independently. The correlation is bounded between -1 and 1, so V1 is the leading term when V2 is much smaller than V1. The correction costs roughly the fine contribution plus extra coarse work that cannot be reused; when that extra work is small, dollar_2 is close to the direct fine cost. Substituting V approximately V1 and dollar approximately dollar_2 into the exact cost ratio gives the displayed expression. These are conditional approximations in the continuous IID work model, not guaranteed savings for every hierarchy. Setup, pilots, and rounding still need to be counted in an implemented comparison.

Slope and curvature give a local model

First take \(m=1\): approximate a small displacement \(s\) from the current \(\theta\)

\[\begin{gather*} \operatorname{Loss}(\theta+s)\approx q(s) =\operatorname{Loss}(\theta)+g s+\tfrac12 h s^2 \\ g=\operatorname{Loss}'(\theta) \qquad h=\operatorname{Loss}''(\theta) \end{gather*}\]

  • Slope \(g\): which direction is locally downhill?
  • Curvature \(h\): how quickly does that slope change?
  • If \(h>0\), the quadratic model is minimized at \(s_{\mathrm{Newton}}=-g/h\)
  • A nonquadratic loss can depart from this model away from the current point
  • Update \(\theta\gets\theta+s\) and rebuild; successive iterations converge to the true minimizer under suitable conditions
  • For nonconvex losses, iterations may miss the global minimum

First and second derivatives help choose a step; they do not describe the whole loss

Differentiate q(s): q’(s)=g+hs, giving its minimizing displacement -g/h when h>0. This is Newton’s step in one dimension. If h=0 the formula is undefined; if h<0 the quadratic has a maximum instead of a minimum. Even with h>0, a full Newton step need not decrease a nonquadratic loss; damping, line searches or trust regions can safeguard it. A zero slope is a stationary point, which need not be a global minimizer in general. Recompute the local model at every new iterate. General convergence claims require conditions on the loss, starting point and step selection; local Newton convergence near a nondegenerate minimizer is not a general global-minimum guarantee for nonconvex losses.

A nonquadratic loss: useful but imperfect curvature

\[\operatorname{Loss}(\theta)=\tfrac12\theta^2+\tfrac14\theta^4 \qquad \operatorname{Loss}'(1)=2 \qquad \operatorname{Loss}''(1)=4\]

The quadratic model chooses \(\theta=1-2/4=1/2\); the true minimizer is \(0\)

The true loss is smooth and strictly convex, but nonquadratic. At theta=1, its value, first derivative and second derivative are 3/4, 2 and 4. The Taylor quadratic in the displacement s is 3/4+2s+2s^2; its minimizer is s=-1/2. At the recommended new theta=1/2, the model predicts loss 1/4 but the actual loss is 9/64=0.140625. Both are different from the true minimum zero. Thus exact local derivatives still give only local information. This example has changing curvature without nonconvexity. Its Hessian is unbounded on the whole real line, so later global L-smooth results are not asserted globally for it. The quartic is used again in the notebook opening. Convexity is a statement about the whole loss

In one variable, a convex loss lies below the line segment between any two points on its graph

\[\operatorname{Loss}((1-t)\theta+t\phi) \leq(1-t)\operatorname{Loss}(\theta)+t\operatorname{Loss}(\phi) \qquad 0\leq t\leq1\]

  • For a differentiable convex loss, zero slope identifies a global minimizer
  • A convex loss can still have several minimizers
  • A nonquadratic loss can be convex: \(\operatorname{Loss}(\theta)=\theta^2+\theta^4\) is an example
  • For a nonconvex loss, zero slope need not identify a global minimum

Changing curvature and nonconvexity are different questions

The same line-segment definition applies to vector arguments in several variables; replace zero slope by zero gradient. Convexity is a global line-segment property. Several variables: a gradient vector

For \(\vtheta=[\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_m]^\top\), the gradient collects the slopes

\[\begin{gather*} \vg=\nabla\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta) =\left[\frac{\partial\operatorname{Loss}}{\partial\theta_1},\ldots, \frac{\partial\operatorname{Loss}}{\partial\theta_m}\right]^\top \\ \operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta+\vs)\approx\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta)+\vg^\top\vs \end{gather*}\]

  • \(m\) is the number of variables or parameters
  • Direction \(\vs\): the local change is described by \(\vg^\top\vs\)
  • Negative gradient: steepest local decrease for a fixed Euclidean step length

Among displacement vectors of norm epsilon, Cauchy–Schwarz minimizes the linear term at s=-epsilon*g/||g|| when g is nonzero. Thus the gradient gives a locally steepest direction in the stated Euclidean geometry. Rescaling variables changes this geometry and can change the path. We retain m for parameter count. When sampling is introduced later, attach d explicitly to the random index vector being drawn. The loss need not involve data.

Several variables: a Hessian matrix

The Hessian describes curvature along and between directions

\[\begin{gather*} \mH=\nabla^2\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta) \qquad H_{j\ell}=\frac{\partial^2\operatorname{Loss}}{\partial\theta_j\partial\theta_\ell} \\ q(\vs)=\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta)+\vg^\top\vs+\tfrac12\vs^\top\mH\vs \\ \mH\vs_{\mathrm{Newton}}=-\vg \quad\text{minimizes }q\text{ when }\mH\text{ is positive definite} \end{gather*}\]

  • Mixed derivatives couple changes in different variables
  • One quadratic now has \(1+m+m(m+1)/2\) independent coefficients
  • Recovering the full model from values alone becomes much harder

The coefficient count uses a symmetric Hessian, as holds for a twice continuously differentiable scalar loss. In one dimension the count is three; in m dimensions it grows quadratically. Fitting an unrestricted quadratic from scalar function values requires at least that many independent conditions and well-chosen points. Newton solves a linear system rather than forming an explicit inverse. A singular or indefinite Hessian needs safeguards; even a positive-definite model may require damping on a nonquadratic loss.

Gradient descent uses first derivatives

Move along the negative gradient with a positive learning rate

\[\vtheta_{k+1}=\vtheta_k-\eta_k\nabla\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_k)\]

  • No full Hessian is required
  • Actual step length: \(\eta_k\norm{\nabla\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_k)}_2\)
  • A short enough step decreases a smooth loss when its gradient is nonzero
  • Too short a step slows down convergence
  • A single learning rate must accommodate different curvatures in different directions

Use the learning rate to control how far to move

This is gradient descent, a first-order method. Its O(m) vector update is in addition to computing the gradient. A zero gradient establishes stationarity, not in general global optimality. Curvature still matters to the step even when the algorithm does not explicitly form a Hessian.

In one dimension, gradient descent follows the slope

\[\begin{gather*} \theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta_k\operatorname{Loss}'(\theta_k) \\ \operatorname{Loss}(\theta+s)\approx\operatorname{Loss}(\theta) +\operatorname{Loss}'(\theta)s \end{gather*}\]

  • Positive slope: move left; negative slope: move right
  • The learning rate determines how far; a large step can increase the loss
  • Newton uses the second derivative as well; GD can use a chosen learning rate

From coordinate contraction to norm contraction

Write \(\vtheta_k=\sum_{j=1}^m c_{k,j}\vv_j\) in an orthonormal eigenbasis, \(\mA\vv_j=\lambda_j\vv_j\)

\(c_{k,j}=\vv_j^\top\vtheta_k\): error coordinate along \(\vv_j\) at iteration \(k\)

\[\begin{gather*} c_{k+1,j}=(1-\eta\lambda_j)c_{k,j} \qquad q(\eta)=\max_j|1-\eta\lambda_j| \\ \norm{\vtheta_{k+1}}_2^2 =\sum_{j=1}^m c_{k+1,j}^2 =\sum_{j=1}^m (1-\eta\lambda_j)^2c_{k,j}^2 \\ \leq q(\eta)^2\sum_{j=1}^m c_{k,j}^2 =q(\eta)^2\norm{\vtheta_k}_2^2 \end{gather*}\]

Take square roots, then repeat with the same step size

\[\begin{gather*} \norm{\vtheta_{k+1}}_2\leq q(\eta)\norm{\vtheta_k}_2 \qquad \norm{\vtheta_k}_2\leq q(\eta)^k\norm{\vtheta_0}_2 \end{gather*}\]

Contraction when \(0<\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}\), giving \(q(\eta)<1\)

Continue the preceding SPD quadratic, with its minimizer at zero. The error vector is expanded in the orthonormal eigenvectors v_j. The scalar c_{k,j} is its signed coordinate in eigenvector direction j at iteration k; it is not another parameter vector. Multiplying the GD update by v_j^T gives the displayed scalar recurrence because v_j^T A=lambda_j v_j^T. Thus the multidimensional iteration becomes separate scalar updates.

The orthonormality identity is Pythagoras: expanding the squared norm of sum_j c_{k,j} v_j produces cross terms v_j^T v_l, which are zero for j != l and one for j=l. Thus ||theta_k||2^2=sum_j c{k,j}^2. This identity is used at both ends of the squared-norm calculation.

Square each scalar coordinate update. Every multiplier squared is at most q(eta)^2; summing preserves the inequality, and taking nonnegative square roots gives the one-step bound. Applying it repeatedly with a fixed eta gives the k-step bound. A strict contraction requires 0 < eta < 2/lambda_max. Equality in the one-step bound is possible for an error in an eigenvector direction attaining the maximum absolute multiplier. No independence or random averaging is used. Balancing the extreme eigenvalue directions

Choose \(\eta\) to minimize the norm-bound factor established above

\[\begin{gather*} q(\eta)=\max_j|1-\eta\lambda_j| =\max\left\{\underbrace{1-\eta\lambda_{\min}}_{\text{decreases with }\eta},\ \underbrace{\eta\lambda_{\max}-1}_{\text{increases with }\eta}\right\} \end{gather*}\]

Best constant step: balance the two extreme multipliers

\[\begin{gather*} 1-\eta\lambda_{\min}=-(1-\eta\lambda_{\max}) \quad\implies\quad \eta_{\mathrm{best}}=\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\min}} \\ q(\eta_{\mathrm{best}})=\frac{\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}}{\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\min}} =\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1} \end{gather*}\]

Balance shallow-direction decay against steep-direction overshoot

The preceding slide established q(eta) as the sharp worst-case Euclidean norm factor. For fixed eta > 0, |1-eta*lambda| is convex in lambda; its maximum over the spectrum is attained at one of the two extreme eigenvalues.

For 0 < eta < 2/lambda_max, the worst-case factor is max(1-etalambda_min, etalambda_max-1). The first expression decreases with eta and the second increases. Their intersection minimizes the maximum; outside this interval the factor is at least one. At the intersection the two extreme multipliers are q and -q. If lambda_min=lambda_max, both are zero and there is no alternation. Otherwise the steep component crosses zero on each update while the shallow component keeps its sign. This refers to the error components, not to an increase in the loss.

For diag(1,100), eta_best=2/101 gives multipliers 99/101 and -99/101. For the identity, eta_best=1 gives zero in every direction. Estimation

Single-observation SGD

Estimation

Select \(I_k\) uniformly from \(\{0,\ldots,N-1\}\), independently of previous draws

\[\begin{gather*} \vG_k=\nabla\operatorname{loss}_{I_k}(\vtheta_k) \qquad \vtheta_{k+1}=\vtheta_k-\eta_k\vG_k \\ \Ex[\vG_k\mid\mathcal F_k]=\nabla\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_k) =\frac1N\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\nabla\operatorname{loss}_i(\vtheta_k) \end{gather*}\]

Here \(\mathcal F_k\) contains the previous draws and the current iterate \(\vtheta_k\)

  • \(\vG_k\): an unbiased but noisy estimate of the full gradient \(\nabla\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_k)\)
  • One sampled term estimates the average of \(N\) gradient terms
  • \(\vtheta_k\) is our known current vector; the minimizer \(\vtheta_*\) is generally unknown
  • A particular update may increase the full loss

Estimate the \(N\)-term average gradient with one term, then make one parameter update

The expectation is over the fresh sampled index, holding the history and current iterate fixed. This first algorithm uses exactly one observation gradient per update to estimate the average of all N component gradients. The objective is an average, so the one-term estimator is grad loss_{I_k}, without another factor of 1/N. Its conditional expectation supplies that factor through the uniform draw. An unnormalized sum objective would instead use N times the sampled component gradient. The mini-batch algorithm below generalizes the same symbol G_k to an average of b observation gradients; b=1 recovers this single-observation case. Unbiased but noisy means that the conditional mean is the full gradient, while the realized gradient estimate varies with the sampled observation. A random permutation of the data within an epoch is also common, but its next index is not independent uniform conditional on all previous indices. Our identities and notebook experiments use sampling with replacement. They are not proofs for arbitrary shuffling or QMC substitutions.

The sampled gradient is a Monte Carlo estimate

Generate \(U\sim\Unif[0,1)\) and select \(I=\lfloor NU\rfloor\)

\[\begin{gather*} \boldsymbol h_{\vtheta}(u)=\nabla\operatorname{loss}_{\lfloor Nu\rfloor}(\vtheta) \\ \int_0^1\boldsymbol h_{\vtheta}(u)\,\dif u =\frac1N\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\nabla\operatorname{loss}_i(\vtheta) =\nabla\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta) \end{gather*}\]

  • Sampled index \(I\): \(d=1\)
  • Output: an \(m\)-component gradient vector
  • One uniform chooses a whole observation, including all its features

A scalar random index selects an \(m\)-component gradient

The vector-valued integral is interpreted componentwise. With a fixed dataset, its randomness is the observation index, not a fresh simulation of all feature coordinates. The chosen step-function integrand is constant on N intervals. Changing the data order changes this cube representation without changing the finite average. Low nominal dimension alone does not imply a smooth QMC integrand or a useful QMC advantage. All identities in this section use fresh IID uniforms; arbitrary QMC or shuffled-epoch substitutions change the conditional dependence and need their own analysis. Values at u=1 are irrelevant to the integral; our index construction draws from [0,1).

Mini-batch SGD and gradient noise

Estimation

A mini-batch uses \(b\) fresh IID observation indices, sampled with replacement

Average their gradients at the current \(\vtheta_k\), then make one update

\[\begin{gather*} \vG_k=\frac1b\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}\nabla\operatorname{loss}_{I_{kj}}(\vtheta_k) \qquad \vtheta_{k+1}=\vtheta_k-\eta_k\vG_k \\ \Ex[\vG_k\mid\mathcal F_k]=\nabla\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_k) \end{gather*}\]

  • After sampling, \(\vG_k\) is a computed vector estimating the full gradient
  • Evaluate every batch gradient at the same current iterate
  • Direct gradient work: \(O(bm)\) instead of \(O(Nm)\) for the full gradient
  • Larger batch: more work per update and less gradient variability

\(b=1\) recovers single-observation SGD; choose \(b\) for accuracy per unit cost

Mini-batch SGD generalizes the preceding single-observation algorithm. Every component gradient is evaluated at the same current theta_k; average them, then make one parameter update. With b=1 and I_{k0}=I_k, both the gradient definition and the parameter update reduce to the preceding slide. Linearity of conditional expectation gives E[G_k | F_k]=grad Loss(theta_k) for every batch size b, because each fresh IID draw is uniform conditional on the history. The estimate is unbiased but generally noisy; averaging reduces its conditional mean squared error without changing its mean.

Expand the squared norm of the average of b centered independent vectors. Cross terms have zero conditional expectation; the b diagonal terms give sigma_k^2/b. Sigma_k^2 is the trace of the component-gradient covariance, not the loss variance. Sampling b distinct observations uniformly without replacement gives an additional (N-b)/(N-1) factor for N>1. Drawing b=N with replacement is not the full gradient; full GD explicitly visits every observation once. Vectorized batches can use hardware more efficiently than single observations; fewer updates alone do not establish lower total work or elapsed time.

\(d=b\) Dimension of the random index vector \(\boldsymbol I\) we draw

The sampling unit here is the whole random index vector needed for one gradient estimate. Each coordinate is independently uniform on the N observation indices. What does gradient noise measure?

At a fixed current iterate, define the component-gradient variability

\[\begin{gather*} \vg_k=\nabla\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_k) \qquad \sigma_k^2=\frac1N\sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \norm{\nabla\operatorname{loss}_i(\vtheta_k)-\vg_k}_2^2 \\ \Ex\bigl[\norm{\vG_k-\vg_k}_2^2\mid\mathcal F_k\bigr] =\frac{\sigma_k^2}{b} \end{gather*}\]

  • This is variability of gradient vectors, not loss values
  • Fresh IID draws: averaging \(b\) gradients divides the variance by \(b\)
  • Individual gradients may remain nonzero at the full loss’s minimizer

Less noise per update costs more observation gradients per update

Sigma_k^2 is the trace of the covariance of one component gradient. Expand the squared norm of the mean of b centered independent vectors: cross terms have zero conditional expectation and b equal diagonal terms remain. For a uniformly selected batch of b distinct observations, multiply the displayed sigma_k^2/b by (N-b)/(N-1), for N>1. This assumes a fresh uniform subset at the current iterate, not the remaining part of a shuffled epoch. With replacement, b=N can repeat or omit observations and is not the full gradient. Full GD visits every observation exactly once.

Estimate noise from one mini-batch

For \(b>1\), let \(\vg_{kj}=\nabla\operatorname{loss}_{I_{kj}}(\vtheta_k)\) and \(\vG_k=b^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}\vg_{kj}\)

\[\begin{gather*} S_k^2=\frac1{b-1}\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}\norm{\vg_{kj}-\vG_k}_2^2 \\ \Ex[S_k^2\mid\mathcal F_k]=\sigma_k^2 \qquad S_k^2/b\text{ estimates batch-gradient noise} \end{gather*}\]

  • Use gradients at the same iterate; one observation cannot supply a sample variance
  • A small batch can give an imprecise variance estimate
  • Variance alone does not reveal curvature or the true full-gradient norm

This is the sum of the coordinate sample variances, so it estimates the trace of the component-gradient covariance without constructing an m-by-m matrix. The identity requires the fresh with-replacement IID sampling used here. The notebook separates exact enumeration of four gradients from estimating variance from one batch, then validates the estimator over repeated batches. Do not pool gradients evaluated at changing theta values as one fixed-iterate sample. For a uniform distinct batch, the usual sample variance estimates the finite-population variance with denominator N-1; its batch-mean variance estimate uses (1-b/N)*S_k^2/b instead.

Smoothness controls the quadratic remainder

How far can we trust the loss change predicted by the slope?

An \(L\)-Lipschitz gradient satisfies

\[\norm{\nabla\operatorname{Loss}(\vx)-\nabla\operatorname{Loss}(\vy)}_2 \leq L\norm{\vx-\vy}_2\]

The descent lemma bounds the error in that local prediction

\[\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta+\vs)\leq\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta) +\nabla\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta)^\top\vs+\frac L2\norm{\vs}_2^2\]

  • A valid \(L\) bounds curvature; the lemma does not find \(L\)
  • With known \(L>0\), full GD at \(\eta=1/L\) decreases the loss when the gradient is nonzero
  • A sampled gradient adds noise: we must account for its effect next

For a twice differentiable function, an operator-norm Hessian bound L on a convex region containing the line segment provides this inequality there. Global statements require a global bound; Rosenbrock is not globally L-smooth on R^2. L is a curvature/smoothness bound, not parameter dimension or gradient noise. Integrating the gradient along the segment proves the displayed descent lemma. Existence of some L does not mean it is known. This lemma is a proved inequality used in the argument that follows. It does not require convexity. For an SPD quadratic, L=lambda_max(A) is valid. For full GD, s=-g/L gives F(theta+s)<=F(theta)-||g||^2/(2L). The analogous SGD calculation has an additional variance term. A measured local curvature can guide tuning without being a valid bound everywhere needed for the proof. The exponential-regression motivation also lacks a finite global L on all of R^m in general. A local argument needs a valid bound on the region containing the proposed step; the later regularized logistic example has an explicitly computable global bound.

Derive expected descent

Hold our known \(\vtheta_k\) fixed and average over possible fresh batches

Choose \(\eta_k\) from past information, before drawing the update batch

\[\begin{gather*} \Ex[\vG_k\mid\mathcal F_k]=\vg_k \qquad \Ex[\norm{\vG_k}_2^2\mid\mathcal F_k]=\norm{\vg_k}_2^2+\sigma_k^2/b \\ \begin{aligned} \Ex[\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_k-\eta_k\vG_k)\mid\mathcal F_k] &\leq\underbrace{\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_k)}_{\text{current loss}} \\ &\quad-\underbrace{\eta_k\norm{\vg_k}_2^2}_{\text{slope prediction}} +\underbrace{\frac{L\eta_k^2}{2}\norm{\vg_k}_2^2}_{\text{curvature allowance}} +\underbrace{\frac{L\eta_k^2\sigma_k^2}{2b}}_{\text{sampling-noise penalty}} \end{aligned} \end{gather*}\]

  • The curvature allowance is present even with the exact gradient
  • Sampling adds the noise penalty; both positive terms require a valid \(L\)

Here g_k is the full gradient defined above and eta_k is measurable with respect to F_k. In E||G||^2, write G=g+(G-g); the mean-zero cross term vanishes. This proof does not apply unchanged when eta_k is computed from the same fresh batch and is therefore correlated with G_k. A separate independent pilot may be included in the pre-update information, but its sampling cost must be charged. The bound is conditional on the current iterate; it is not a statement that every realized step lowers the loss. Substitute s=-eta_k G_k in the descent lemma. The purpose of this calculation is to explain why a cheap noisy direction can still improve the loss on average. It does not tell us the current full gradient or calculate eta_k.

Why a noisy direction can help

With \(0<\eta_k\leq1/L\), write the bound using the full gradient \(\vg_k\)

\[\begin{gather*} \Ex[\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_{k+1})\mid\mathcal F_k] \leq\operatorname{Loss}(\vtheta_k) -\underbrace{\frac{\eta_k}{2}\norm{\vg_k}_2^2}_{\text{downhill progress}} +\underbrace{\frac{L\eta_k^2\sigma_k^2}{2b}}_{\text{noise penalty}} \end{gather*}\]

  • Far from a stationary point, downhill progress can dominate
  • Near it, a fixed learning rate can leave persistent fluctuations
  • Decrease \(\eta_k\) or increase \(b\) when finer accuracy is needed

Expected decrease is certified by this bound when \(L\eta_k\sigma_k^2/b<\norm{\vg_k}_2^2\)

Noisy gradients can be cheap enough to be useful; noise does not cure poor conditioning

The descent lemma gives F(theta-eta G) <= F(theta)-eta grad(F)^T G + L eta^2 ||G||^2/2. Conditional unbiasedness and the squared-norm variance decomposition give -eta(1-L eta/2)||grad(F)||^2+L eta^2 sigma_k^2/(2b). Using eta<=1/L yields the displayed simpler bound. This is an expected one-step bound under the stated assumptions, not descent on every draw or a global-minimum guarantee. Rosenbrock is not globally L-smooth on all of R^2; the bound is not asserted globally for that example. See Bottou, Curtis, and Nocedal, sections 4.1-4.3, for fuller convergence assumptions and results.

What would the bound suggest for a learning rate?

For insight, suppose the costly or unavailable inputs were known: \(a_k=\norm{\vg_k}_2^2>0\), \(v_k=\sigma_k^2/b\), and \(L\)

\[\begin{gather*} \text{Expected change}\ \leq -\eta a_k+\frac L2\eta^2(a_k+v_k) \\ \eta_{\mathrm{bound}}=\frac{a_k}{L(a_k+v_k)} =\frac{1/L}{1+v_k/a_k} \end{gather*}\]

  • This rate minimizes the displayed upper bound; its inputs are not automatically available
  • Little noise relative to signal: approach the conservative step \(1/L\)
  • Larger noise-to-signal ratio: smaller step, or a larger batch

This explains why noise calls for smaller steps; practical rate selection still needs tuning

Differentiate the right-hand side with respect to eta: -a+Leta(a+v)=0. The second derivative is L(a+v)>0. The full bound guarantees a strict expected decrease for 0<eta<2a/[L(a+v)]. Eta_bound lies at most 1/L, so it is also compatible with the simplified preceding bound. The upper-bound minimizer need not minimize the true conditional expected loss. It differs from the best constant SPD-quadratic norm contraction step 2/(lambda_min+lambda_max): these optimize different criteria and use different information. If a=0 and v>0, the bound predicts only a noise penalty; if both vanish, the formula is undefined but the update gradient is zero almost surely. The exact v_k=sigma_k^2/b can be estimated by S_k^2/b for b>1. Replacing unknown inputs by estimates introduces uncertainty and does not make the formula a guaranteed or implemented learning-rate selector.

What noise estimates cannot choose on their own

\[\begin{gather*} \Ex[\norm{\vG_k}_2^2\mid\mathcal F_k] =\norm{\vg_k}_2^2+\sigma_k^2/b \\ \norm{\vG_k}_2^2-S_k^2/b \quad\text{is an unbiased, but possibly negative, signal estimate} \end{gather*}\]

  • Batch variance estimates noise, not curvature \(L\) or an exact gradient signal
  • A small sample may give an unreliable noise-to-signal ratio
  • Use past information or an independent pilot for noise-informed decisions

The descent bound explains the tradeoff; it does not supply unknown inputs

Subtracting two unbiased expectations gives the signal estimate, but a realization can be negative. Blindly inserting it into eta_bound is not a reliable selection algorithm. Choosing eta from the same update batch also breaks the pre-draw measurability used in the simple expected-descent proof. Pilots and previous iterations avoid that particular dependence, while estimation uncertainty, moving curvature and changing noise still remain. For a deterministic full gradient, backtracking can test actual sufficient decrease. A noisy batch loss need not certify decrease of the full loss.

Estimation

  3. Repeat: draw a fresh batch, average its gradients at \(\vtheta_k\), and update

\[\vtheta_{k+1}=\vtheta_k-\eta_k\vG_k\]

  1. Monitor loss periodically on common diagnostic data; count that cost

Learning rates and the noise floor

A scalar example: \(\operatorname{loss}_i(\theta)=\tfrac12(\theta-a_i)^2\) with \(a_i\in\{-1,1\}\) equally likely

This squared-error average has \(\operatorname{Loss}(\theta)=\tfrac12(\theta^2+1)\) and \(\theta_*=0\)

Let \(\overline a_k\) be the mean of the \(b\) independently sampled responses in batch \(k\)

\[\begin{gather*} \operatorname{loss}'_i(\theta)=\theta-a_i \qquad \theta_{k+1}=(1-\eta)\theta_k+\eta\overline a_k \\ \Ex(\overline a_k)=0 \qquad \var(\overline a_k)=1/b \\ s_k=\Ex(\theta_k^2) \quad\implies\quad s_{k+1}=(1-\eta)^2s_k+\eta^2/b \end{gather*}\]

Even at \(\theta_*=0\), the two observation gradients are \(+1\) and \(-1\)

The a_i are the observed response values in this finite-sum example. Each batch independently selects responses with replacement. Conditional on theta_k, its average has mean zero and variance 1/b, so the cross term in the squared update averages to zero. Full GD uses the exact mean zero, while SGD inserts a noisy batch mean. This recurrence is exact; it does not require the conservative eta<=1/L restriction used earlier. Here L=1, and the moment recurrence contracts for the wider range 0<eta<2.

Persistent noise gives a floor

For constant \(0<\eta<2\) and independent batches,

\[\begin{gather*} s_\infty=(1-\eta)^2s_\infty+\eta^2/b \quad\implies\quad \lim_{k\to\infty}\Ex(\theta_k^2)=\frac{\eta}{b(2-\eta)} \\ \operatorname{Loss}(\theta)-\operatorname{Loss}(0)=\theta^2/2 \end{gather*}\]

  • Smaller \(\eta\) or larger \(b\): lower limiting mean squared error
  • Decaying \(\eta_k\): progressively smaller updates, slower late progress
  • A classical schedule is \(\eta_k=\eta_0/(1+k/k_0)\)
    Convergence still needs assumptions on the objective and noise
  • This floor is not inevitable if all component gradients vanish at the solution

Choose an action by simulating futures

Monte Carlo
Tree Search

At the current state, choose an action with a high expected return

  • A node represents a state or decision history; branches represent possible actions
  • A simulator generates possible continuations and their rewards
  • Grow the tree where additional simulations are most useful
  • Applications include game moves and sequential planning

Monte Carlo estimates guide which action to take and where to search next

MCTS means Monte Carlo tree search, not Markov chain tree search. It is a sequential-decision and search method, distinct from MCMC target sampling. For this introduction use a finite-horizon, single-agent planning problem with bounded rewards and a simulator for state transitions. A return is the sum of future rewards, with discounting if specified. A fixed rollout policy estimates its own continuation value, not automatically the optimal value; continued tree expansion and improved decisions matter. Stochastic transitions can be represented by chance nodes or sampled within the simulator. Source: Kocsis and Szepesvari (2006).

One tree-search iteration

Monte Carlo
Tree Search
Step What happens
Select Follow branches using estimated returns and exploration bonuses
Expand Add an untried action or a new node when possible
Simulate Complete a rollout to a terminal state or chosen horizon
Backpropagate Update visit counts and average returns along the traversed path

Repeat until the simulation budget is exhausted, then choose a root action

Allocate simulations adaptively; avoid exploring every branch equally

Backpropagation here means updating tree statistics, not differentiating through a neural network. Each ancestor uses the return appropriate to its position on the path, including rewards and discounting when relevant. Terminal states skip expansion and rollout. A truncated rollout may use a value approximation, adding modeling error. A common final choice is the most visited root action; distinguish that decision rule from the exploratory selection rule used during tree growth. Parallel rollouts are possible, but MCTS is a search strategy with its own separate teaching purpose.

Exploration versus exploitation

Monte Carlo
Tree Search

An illustrative UCT selection score for visited actions is

\[ \overline Q(s,a)+c\sqrt{\frac{\log N(s)}{N(s,a)}} \qquad c>0 \]

  • \(\overline Q(s,a)\): estimated return — exploit promising actions
  • \(N(s)\) and \(N(s,a)\): state and action visit counts
  • The bonus favors less visited actions — explore uncertain alternatives

For \(N(s)=60\) and \(c=0.2\),

Action \(N(s,a)\) \(\overline Q(s,a)\) Selection score
A 40 0.70 0.764
B 10 0.60 0.728
C 10 0.68 0.808

\(\exstar\) Which action gets the next simulation, and why is it different from the largest estimated return?

Choose C, despite A having the largest current mean. Its exploration bonus is about 0.128 versus about 0.064 for A. Visit untried actions before applying the formula so no division by zero occurs. The natural logarithm is used; rewards are scaled to [0,1] for this illustration. UCT means upper confidence bounds applied to trees. The exploration parameter c depends on reward scale and the chosen UCT convention. This is an illustrative selection score, not a confidence interval for an arbitrary adaptively generated tree return. Tree return distributions evolve as the search policy changes; ordinary IID standard-error reasoning does not automatically apply. Original UCT results require assumptions such as bounded rewards and finite-horizon or discounted planning. No finite-budget optimal-action guarantee is claimed. Source: Kocsis and Szepesvari, Bandit based Monte-Carlo Planning (2006).
